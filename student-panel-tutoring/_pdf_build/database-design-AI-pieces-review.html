 SECTION 1 - PIECE 2 (THE SAMPLES PDF): IS IT ENOUGH?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

 Enough for its job: YES. Its job is to SHOW, as real tables and diagrams, every
 idea the notebook teaches in prose. It does that well across 19 lessons mapped
 to Parts 1-7 of the shared material: entity-vs-attribute, key attributes,
 ternary relationships, enrolment/course-offering, the noun/property/verb to
 entity/attribute/relationship translation, referential integrity, foreign-key
 placement, the order/order_line bridge, the five hard patterns, 1NF/2NF/3NF
 with anomalies, a full worked gym design, eight self-check tests, and a
 glossary. Each sample has concrete rows, which is exactly what a beginner needs
 to turn a rule into a picture.

 Where it goes BEYOND my primer (useful additions - keep these):
   + THE FROZEN-FACT EXCEPTION. My primer says "derive, don't store." Piece 2
     (Lesson 16) adds the important exception: a value captured on purpose at a
     moment in time - unit_price_at_sale on an order line - is NOT a derived
     value you should drop; it is a frozen historical fact you must keep. My
     primer only hints at this; Piece 2 states it clearly. GOOD addition.
   + RESERVED WORDS. Piece 2 warns that "order" and "group" are reserved words
     in SQL and must be named safely (orders, groups). My primer never mentions
     this. It is a real physical-level gotcha and matters for student-panel
     (Postgres), so it is a worthwhile addition.
   + THE EXPLICIT CROW'S-FOOT MIRRORING. Piece 2 spells out that the symbols are
     mirrored on the two ends of a line (o< on one side, >o on the other, same
     meaning). My primer USES this convention but never explains it, which can
     confuse a beginner. Piece 2's note is a helpful addition (I folded it into
     the companion lesson, Part 3).
   + A SURROGATE-ID OPTION FOR BRIDGES. For the order_line bridge, Piece 2 notes
     you may either use the pair as the key OR give the bridge its own single id
     AND keep a UNIQUE on the pair. My primer mostly shows only the pair-as-key
     style. Piece 2's extra option is valid and worth knowing.
   + TWO SMALL HEURISTICS. The "disguise test" for a ternary ("drop one noun -
     is the sentence still a real fact?") and a beginner-friendly definition of
     dependency ("A depends on B when knowing B tells you A"). Both are nice
     teaching aids my primer does not phrase as crisply.

 What it is MISSING / needs added (one real item):
   - UUID / student-panel's key style. Piece 2 uses INTEGER auto-numbers (1, 2,
     3) for every primary key and never mentions UUIDs. student-panel uses UUID
     primary keys plus a unique human code. A learner who only read Piece 2
     would be surprised by student-panel's long random ids. This is the one
     addition I would insist on (details in Section 3, difference #1).

 SECTION 3 - DIFFERENCES: BOTH SIDES, AND WHICH IS CORRECT
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

 Here is every place the pieces differ from my lesson or from each other. For
 each: both sides, the verdict, and the reason. None of these is a flat-out
 contradiction where one side is simply wrong; they are differences of CHOICE or
 EMPHASIS - but you asked me to hide nothing, so each is laid out in full.

 DIFFERENCE #1  -  INTEGER ids (pieces) vs UUID + natural code (my primer and
                   student-panel).
   Piece side:  every primary key is an INTEGER auto-number: member_id 1, 2, 3.
   My side:     recommend a surrogate key too, but student-panel (and my primer)
                use a UUID (a long random id) PLUS a unique human-readable code.
   Which is correct: BOTH are correct - they are two kinds of the SAME idea (a
   surrogate key invented by the system). Neither contradicts the other.
   Why / when each wins: INTEGER ids are smaller, easier to read, and perfect
   for learning. UUIDs win when ids must be unique across many machines, or must
   not reveal how many rows exist or in what order they were made. student-panel
   chose UUID; the pieces chose INT for teaching clarity.
   What to DO: just know the two styles are interchangeable in concept. When you
   see "1, 2, 3" in the pieces and long random ids in student-panel, that is the
   same decision in two costumes - NOT a conflict. (This is the one item the
   pieces should ADD a sentence about; see Section 1.)

 DIFFERENCE #2  -  the two TEN-STEP design methods (my primer's Part 7 vs
                   Piece 2's Lesson 15).
   Piece 2 even flags this itself: "This routine is my own. Your primer has its
   own Part 7. Read it later and compare the two lists." So the difference is
   invited, not accidental. Side by side:

     MY PRIMER (Part 7)                 THE PIECES (Lesson 15)
     1 State the purpose (+ questions)  1 Purpose
     2 Find the entities                2 Questions and actions
     3 Find the attributes              3 Entities
     4 Find the relationships           4 Attributes and keys
     5 Choose keys                      5 Say the pairs aloud (cardinality)
     6 Draw it                          6 Find the rich links (M:N/ternary/facts)
     7 Resolve the hard patterns        7 Place the foreign keys
     8 Normalise (3NF)                  8 Choose the delete rules
     9 Map to tables (+ delete rules)   9 Check normal forms
     10 Review against the purpose      10 Test with rows

   Which is correct: BOTH - they are the SAME method sliced differently, and
   they reach the same destination. Mapping them:
     - My step 1 bundles purpose+questions; the pieces split them (their 1-2).
     - My step 5 "keys" is separate; the pieces fold keys into "attributes and
       keys" (their 4).
     - My step 6 is an explicit "Draw it"; the pieces' list has no draw step
       (though the whole PDF is drawings).
     - My step 7 "resolve hard patterns" = their 6 "rich links" + 7 "place FKs".
     - My step 9 bundles delete rules; the pieces make "delete rules" their own
       step 8.
     - Both end by testing against reality (my 10 "review"; their 10 "rows").
   Verdict / what to DO: use whichever ordering you find easier - they are
   equivalent. The BEST routine borrows the strengths of each: keep my explicit
   "Draw it" step, and adopt the pieces' idea of making "questions/actions" and
   "delete rules" their OWN steps so a beginner cannot skip them. (I will build
   exactly this merged, numbered routine into the corrected final files.)

 DIFFERENCE #3  -  a surrogate-keyed ternary WITHOUT an explicit UNIQUE on the
                   natural combination (an inconsistency INSIDE the pieces).
   The pieces are careful about this in one place and silent in another:
     - For the order_line bridge they explicitly say: if you give the bridge its
       own id, keep a UNIQUE on the pair so you cannot store the same pairing
       twice. GOOD.
     - For the prescription ternary (doctor + drug + patient, with its own
       prescription_id) they do NOT mention putting a UNIQUE on the natural
       triple.
   Is this a contradiction? Mild, and internal to the pieces, not against my
   primer. Which is correct: the order_line advice is the fully-correct pattern.
   Why: if a surrogate id is the only key, nothing stops two identical pairings/
   triples unless you ALSO constrain the natural combination - UNLESS the real
   world genuinely allows repeats (e.g. the same doctor may prescribe the same
   drug to the same patient again on a later date, in which case the triple is
   deliberately NOT unique and a date belongs in the key instead). What to DO:
   whenever you give a bridge or ternary its own id, make a conscious decision -
   "should the natural combination be UNIQUE?" - and write it down. The pieces
   model this well for order_line; they should say the same for prescription.
   This is a small "needs a note," not an error.

 DIFFERENCE #4  -  two models shown for the same relationship, without a strong
                   "pick this one when..." (an emphasis gap, not a conflict).
   The pieces show BOTH a course_offering with its own surrogate id AND a
   "teaching" bridge with a composite (course, semester) key for essentially the
   same situation, and both the pair-key and surrogate-id bridge styles for
   order_line. All of these are correct. The small gap: a beginner may not know
   WHICH to choose. Verdict: not wrong, but my corrected files will add a short
   "choose the pair-key when the pairing is naturally unique and you never point
   at it from elsewhere; choose a surrogate id when something else must
   reference the row, and keep a UNIQUE on the pair."

 NON-DIFFERENCES (places they fully AGREE with my primer - so you can relax):
   = 1NF/2NF/3NF, the three anomalies, and the mnemonic "the key, the whole key,
     and nothing but the key" - identical.
   = Foreign key goes on the MANY side; 1:1 FK on the never-empty side with
     UNIQUE+NOT NULL - identical (same passport example).
   = Self-reference tree with a parent pointer and NULL at the root; and the
     reason not to use parent/grandparent columns - identical.
   = ON DELETE RESTRICT / CASCADE / SET NULL meanings - identical (the pieces
     add a nice "say the rule as a sentence" habit, which is pure upside).
   = The "one move" - M:N, a link with its own facts, and a three-way fact all
     become a table in the middle - identical to my Part 5.

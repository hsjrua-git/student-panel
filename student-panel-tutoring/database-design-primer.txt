This primer teaches the CRAFT of designing a database: how you get from "here is
a situation in the real world" to "here are the tables, and here is why they
look like this." It uses small, everyday examples - a library, a school, a shop
- on purpose. student-panel is an advanced, finished design; you will design far
better once you have built the method here first, on things small enough to hold
in your head.

Nothing here asks you to touch a computer. Design happens on paper, in your head,
in conversation. Read slowly. Keep a pencil and a blank sheet beside you.

A NOTE ON THIS EDITION. This is the fully-drawn edition. Wherever an idea can be
shown as a table of real rows or as a diagram, it is drawn, not just described -
because you learn design by SEEING data sit in its places and by seeing lines
connect boxes. Everything is hand-drawn in plain characters. Read this file in a
MONOSPACE (fixed-width) font - the one your code editor uses - or the tables and
diagrams will not line up and the crow's-foot marks will drift. If a box looks
crooked, your font is proportional; switch it. Nothing here is decorative; every
table and every diagram is there to be read, row by row and mark by mark.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  PART 1 - WHAT "DESIGNING A DATABASE" ACTUALLY MEANS  (the biggest picture)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Start as high up as we can. A database is a place that remembers facts for you,
in an organised way, so a program can store them and ask questions of them
later. Designing one means deciding WHAT facts you will keep and HOW they are
arranged. That is it. Everything technical below serves those two decisions.

Here is the mental move at the centre of all of it: a database is a small,
deliberate MODEL of a slice of the real world. The real world is infinitely
detailed; your model keeps only the parts your program cares about and throws
the rest away. A library's database "knows" a book has a title and an author,
but not that its cover is scuffed or that it smells of coffee. Design is the art
of choosing what to keep.

WHY NOT JUST ONE BIG SPREADSHEET?
  Because facts repeat, and repetition rots. Put a member's phone number next to
  every book they borrow, and the day they change numbers you must find and fix
  it in twenty places - miss one and your data now disagrees with itself. A good
  design stores each fact ONCE, in the one place it belongs, and links to it from
  everywhere else. Most of this primer is really about that single sentence.

  Here is the rot, drawn. One flat sheet, one row per borrowing, the member's
  details copied onto every row:

  +---------+-------------+--------------+-----------+------------+
  | loan_id | member_name | member_phone | book      | due_date   |
  +---------+-------------+--------------+-----------+------------+
  | 1       | Sara Adib   | 555-1020     | Dune      | 2026-10-14 |
  | 2       | Sara Adib   | 555-1020     | Emma      | 2026-10-14 |
  | 3       | Omar Reyes  | 555-7788     | Dune      | 2026-10-20 |
  | 4       | Sara Adib   | 555-9999 (!) | The Hobbit| 2026-10-28 |
  +---------+-------------+--------------+-----------+------------+

  Look at row 4: someone updated Sara's phone there but not on rows 1 and 2. Now
  the sheet claims Sara has TWO different phone numbers and cannot say which is
  right. Nobody lied; the shape of the sheet made the mistake possible. The whole
  of Part 6 is about making that mistake impossible, by storing Sara's phone in
  exactly one place and pointing at it. For now just feel the ache of row 4.


THE THREE LEVELS OF A DESIGN
  Designers deliberately think at three heights, from ideas down to storage:
    1. CONCEPTUAL - the ideas and their connections, in plain language, with no
       thought of any particular database product. "A member borrows many
       copies; a copy belongs to one book." This is where ER modelling lives.
    2. LOGICAL - those ideas turned into tables, columns, keys and links, still
       without worrying which database engine runs it.
    3. PHYSICAL - the real thing on a real engine: exact data types, indexes,
       storage. This is last, and it is the least interesting part of design.
  We spend almost all our time at levels 1 and 2. Beginners rush to level 3
  (typing commands); good designers linger at level 1 (getting the ideas right).

  The same idea, shown descending through the three heights:
  +------------+--------------------------------+--------------------------------+
  | level      | what you say                   | library example                |
  +------------+--------------------------------+--------------------------------+
  | conceptual | nouns + verbs, plain words     | "a member borrows copies"      |
  | logical    | tables, columns, keys, links   | loan(loan_id, member_id FK,    |
  |            |                                | copy_id FK, borrowed_on)       |
  | physical   | types, sizes, indexes, engine  | loan_id BIGINT, index on       |
  |            |                                | member_id, Postgres            |
  +------------+--------------------------------+--------------------------------+

WHERE "ER" COMES FROM
  In 1976 Peter Chen proposed describing data as ENTITIES (things) joined by
  RELATIONSHIPS (how the things relate). It stuck because it matches how people
  already talk: nouns and verbs. An ER diagram is just a picture of nouns and
  verbs. You already think this way; we are only making it precise.

  KEY IDEA: a database is a chosen model of reality; design is deciding what to
  keep and how to arrange it so every fact lives in exactly one place.


WHAT MAKES A DESIGN "GOOD"?
  Four things, roughly in order of importance:
    - FAITHFUL: it can represent every real situation you care about, and it
      forbids situations that can't really happen (a loan with no book).
    - NON-REDUNDANT: each fact is stored once, so it can never contradict itself.
    - ANSWERABLE: the questions you will actually ask are easy and fast to ask.
    - SIMPLE: no table, column, or link exists without a reason you can state.
  Notice these can pull against each other - chasing zero redundancy can make
  questions harder to ask. Design is the judgement that balances them. There is
  rarely one "right" schema, only better and worse trade-offs for a purpose.

  Keep this scorecard beside you and test any design against it:
  +---------------+--------------------------------+----------------------------+
  | property      | the test question              | failing symptom            |
  +---------------+--------------------------------+----------------------------+
  | faithful      | can it store every real case,  | you can't record a true    |
  |               | and refuse impossible ones?    | situation; or you can store|
  |               |                                | a nonsense one             |
  | non-redundant | is each fact in exactly 1 place| the same fact in two rows  |
  |               |                                | can disagree (row 4 above) |
  | answerable    | are my real questions easy?    | a common question needs a  |
  |               |                                | monstrous query            |
  | simple        | can I name a reason for each   | a table nobody can explain |
  |               | table and column?              |                            |
  +---------------+--------------------------------+----------------------------+

BEGIN FROM THE QUESTIONS, NOT THE TABLES
  The single most useful habit: before drawing anything, write down the questions
  the system must answer and the actions it must support. "Which books is this
  member holding right now?" "How many copies of this title do we own?" "Who had
  this book before it was returned damaged?" A design is good exactly to the
  degree it answers ITS questions well. Tables you can't tie back to a real
  question are usually tables that shouldn't exist.


  >> CONTEMPLATE: imagine a tiny system for a coffee shop's loyalty cards. Before
     reading on, write five questions it must answer and two actions it must
     support. Keep the list - we test designs against lists like this.

  A WORKED VERSION (peek only after you have written your own).
     Here is one reasonable answer, written the way Step 1 of the method (Part 7)
     wants it. Yours will differ - that is fine; the point is the HABIT.
     +----+--------------------------------------------------------------+
     | #  | QUESTION the loyalty system must answer                      |
     +----+--------------------------------------------------------------+
     | Q1 | how many stars does customer C have right now?               |
     | Q2 | which rewards has C already redeemed?                        |
     | Q3 | how many stars were earned shop-wide last week?              |
     | Q4 | is this card number valid and whose is it?                   |
     | Q5 | which customers have not visited in 60 days?                 |
     +----+--------------------------------------------------------------+
     +----+--------------------------------------------------------------+
     | #  | ACTION it must support                                       |
     +----+--------------------------------------------------------------+
     | A1 | record a visit that earns stars                              |
     | A2 | redeem stars for a reward                                    |
     +----+--------------------------------------------------------------+
     Notice already: Q1's "stars right now" smells DERIVED (sum of stars earned
     minus stars spent) rather than a number you store and must keep fresh. Hold
     that thought - it returns in Part 2.2 and Part 9. Good question lists leak
     design hints like this before you have drawn a single box.

  >> PITFALL: starting by asking "what columns do I need?" That is level-3
     thinking wearing a disguise. Start at level 1: what THINGS exist, and how do
     they relate? Columns come later and almost fall out on their own.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  PART 2 - THE THREE PRIMITIVES: ENTITIES, ATTRIBUTES, RELATIONSHIPS
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Almost every ER model is built from just three kinds of thing. Learn these three
deeply and the rest of design is refinement. We take them major-to-minor.

  2.1  ENTITIES - the "things"
  ----------------------------
  An entity is a thing your system needs to remember: a Member, a Book, an Order,
  a Student, a Room. If you can point at it and say "we keep facts about that,"
  it is a candidate entity. In speech, entities are usually the NOUNS.

  Two words you must not confuse:
    - ENTITY TYPE (or entity set): the KIND of thing - "Book". This becomes a
      TABLE. When people say "entity" they usually mean this.
    - ENTITY INSTANCE: one particular one - "Book #4417, 'Dune'". This becomes a
      ROW in that table.
  A table is the type; each row is an instance. Hold that distinction; a lot of
  confusion later is really this confusion.

  Drawn, the type IS the table and each instance IS one row of it:

        entity TYPE "BOOK"  ------------------->  the whole table
        +----------+-----------------+--------------------+
        | book_id  | title           | author             |   <- column = attribute
        +----------+-----------------+--------------------+
        | 4417     | Dune            | Frank Herbert      |   <- one row = one
        | 4418     | Emma            | Jane Austen        |      INSTANCE of BOOK
        | 4419     | The Hobbit      | J.R.R. Tolkien     |
        +----------+-----------------+--------------------+
     "Book" (the kind) is the table. "Book #4417, Dune" (one of them) is a row.


  WHAT IS *NOT* AN ENTITY (yet)
    - A mere property of something else ("a book's title") - that is an ATTRIBUTE,
      not an entity of its own... usually. See the contemplation below; the line
      moves.
    - An action ("borrowing") - that is usually a RELATIONSHIP. But actions worth
      remembering in detail often become entities too (a "Loan"). Judgement again.

  A quick test you can run on any noun you underline. Ask: do we keep facts ABOUT
  this thing itself? If yes, it is an entity; if it is just one fact about another
  thing, it is an attribute.
  +---------------+---------------------------+-----------------+----------------+
  | candidate noun| for this system...        | entity or attr? | why            |
  +---------------+---------------------------+-----------------+----------------+
  | Book          | library: lends books      | ENTITY (table)  | many facts: it |
  |               |                           |                 | has title,isbn |
  | title         | library                   | attribute       | one fact OF a  |
  |               |                           |                 | book           |
  | Publisher     | library: tracks who       | ENTITY (table)  | has name,city, |
  |               | prints each title         |                 | many books     |
  | colour        | car-rental app            | attribute       | one fact OF a  |
  |               |                           |                 | car            |
  | Colour        | paint maker: formulas,    | ENTITY (table)  | has code,      |
  |               | suppliers per colour      |                 | formula, etc.  |
  | borrowing     | library                   | RELATIONSHIP    | a verb joining |
  |               |                           | -> becomes Loan | member & copy  |
  +---------------+---------------------------+-----------------+----------------+

  >> CONTEMPLATE: is COLOUR an entity or an attribute? For a car-rental app,
     colour is probably just an attribute of Car. For a paint manufacturer, Colour
     is a full entity with a code, a formula, a supplier. Same word, different
     model - because the PURPOSE differs. What decides it? Ask: do we keep facts
     ABOUT the colour itself? If yes, it wants to be an entity.

  TRY IT: for a cinema, underline the nouns in "a customer books seats for a
     screening of a film." Which become entities (we keep facts about them), and
     which are just attributes? Which word is a verb hiding a future table?

  KEY IDEA: entity type = table = a kind of thing; entity instance = row = one
  specific thing. Entities are the nouns you keep facts about.


  2.2  ATTRIBUTES - what we know about a thing
  --------------------------------------------
  An attribute is a fact stored about an entity: a Member's name, a Book's title,
  a Student's birthdate. In tables, attributes become COLUMNS. Choosing good
  attributes is choosing what your model notices about the world.

  Attributes come in kinds worth naming, because each changes how you model it:

    - ATOMIC (simple) vs COMPOSITE. Atomic means "one indivisible value" - a
      price, a title. Composite means it is really several values wearing one
      name - "address" is street + city + postcode. Composites usually get split
      into separate columns, because you will want to search or sort by the parts.

    - SINGLE-VALUED vs MULTI-VALUED. A person has one birthdate (single) but may
      have many phone numbers (multi). This one matters enormously: a table cell
      holds ONE value, so a multi-valued attribute CANNOT simply be a column. It
      almost always signals a new entity or a link table. Remember this; it is the
      seed of the most common design pattern of all (Part 5).

    - STORED vs DERIVED. Some facts are stored directly (birthdate); others can be
      COMPUTED from stored ones (age = today - birthdate). Derived facts are best
      NOT stored - you recompute them when asked, so they can never drift out of
      date. "Derive, don't store" is a rule you will meet again and again. (There
      is one deliberate exception - a value you FREEZE on purpose at a moment in
      time, like the price charged on a sale. We meet it in Part 9.)

    - KEY attributes - the ones that identify the thing. Important enough to get
      their own part (Part 3).

  The four kinds on one card, with the modelling move each one forces:
  +----------------+----------------------+--------------------------------------+
  | kind           | example              | what it forces you to do             |
  +----------------+----------------------+--------------------------------------+
  | atomic         | price, title, isbn   | one plain column                     |
  | composite      | address, full name   | split into columns (street, city...) |
  | single-valued  | birthdate            | one plain column                     |
  | multi-valued   | phone numbers        | its OWN rows/table (never phone1,2,3)|
  | stored         | birthdate            | keep it                              |
  | derived        | age, order total     | compute on demand; do NOT store      |
  +----------------+----------------------+--------------------------------------+


  COMPOSITE, DRAWN: split the parts you will search or sort by.
    BAD (one lump you cannot sort by city):
    +----------+-------------------------------------------+
    | memb_id  | address                                   |
    +----------+-------------------------------------------+
    | M-1001   | 14 Rose Lane, Brighton, BN1 2AA           |
    | M-1002   | 7 Oak Street, Leeds, LS1 4DF              |
    +----------+-------------------------------------------+
    BETTER (sort by city, post to a postcode, all easy):
    +----------+---------------+-----------+-----------+
    | memb_id  | street        | city      | postcode  |
    +----------+---------------+-----------+-----------+
    | M-1001   | 14 Rose Lane  | Brighton  | BN1 2AA   |
    | M-1002   | 7 Oak Street  | Leeds     | LS1 4DF   |
    +----------+---------------+-----------+-----------+

  MULTI-VALUED, DRAWN: the classic beginner trap and its cure.
    BAD (fixed columns - what about a 4th phone? and most cells sit empty):
    +----------+-------------+-----------+-----------+-----------+
    | memb_id  | name        | phone1    | phone2    | phone3    |
    +----------+-------------+-----------+-----------+-----------+
    | M-1001   | Sara Adib   | 555-1020  | 555-3344  | (null)    |
    | M-1002   | Omar Reyes  | 555-7788  | (null)    | (null)    |
    +----------+-------------+-----------+-----------+-----------+
    CURE (one row per phone, in its own table, linked back by memb_id):
    +------------+----------+-----------+-----------+
    | phone_id   | memb_id  | number    | kind      |
    +------------+----------+-----------+-----------+
    | P-1        | M-1001   | 555-1020  | mobile    |
    | P-2        | M-1001   | 555-3344  | work      |
    | P-3        | M-1002   | 555-7788  | mobile    |
    +------------+----------+-----------+-----------+
    Now a member can have one phone or ten; nothing sits empty; and you never
    guessed a maximum. This little table is your first glimpse of Part 5.


  DERIVED, DRAWN: store the birthdate, NOT the age.
    You store only the left column. "age" is shown here greyed in parentheses to
    mean "computed when asked, never written down":
    +----------+-------------+-------------+----------------------------+
    | memb_id  | name        | birthdate   | (age - DERIVED, not stored)|
    +----------+-------------+-------------+----------------------------+
    | M-1001   | Sara Adib   | 1998-03-11  | (28, recompute each year)  |
    | M-1002   | Omar Reyes  | 2001-07-02  | (25, recompute each year)  |
    +----------+-------------+-------------+----------------------------+
    If you stored "age = 28" it would be a lie by Sara's next birthday and nobody
    would remember to fix it. The birthdate never goes stale; the age always can.
    So: keep the stable fact, compute the changing one. (The exception - a number
    you deliberately FREEZE at a moment, like a sale price - is in Part 9.)

  >> CONTEMPLATE: "full name" - atomic or composite? If you will ever sort by
     surname or greet someone by first name, it is composite: store the parts.
     If you truly only ever print it whole, atomic is fine. The USE decides.

  >> PITFALL: a column holding "555-1234, 555-9988" (two phone numbers jammed in
     one cell). That is a multi-valued attribute in disguise, and it breaks the
     first rule of tidy tables (Part 6). One value per cell, always.


  2.3  RELATIONSHIPS - how the things connect
  -------------------------------------------
  A relationship is an association between entities: a Member BORROWS a Copy; a
  Book HAS a Publisher; a Student ENROLS IN a Course. In speech, relationships are
  the VERBS joining your nouns.

  A relationship's DEGREE is how many entity types it joins:
    - UNARY (recursive): an entity related to itself. An Employee MANAGES another
      Employee; a Category is the PARENT OF another Category. This is how trees and
      hierarchies are built, and it looks strange the first time - one box with a
      line looping back to itself. (Part 5 returns to it; student-panel leans on
      it heavily.)
    - BINARY: two entity types - by far the most common. Member-Loan, Book-Author.
    - TERNARY (and higher): three at once. "A Doctor prescribes a Drug to a
      Patient" links three things in one fact. These are rarer and usually get
      re-expressed as an entity in the middle (Part 5).

  The three degrees, drawn:

    UNARY (one box, line loops back to itself):
        +---------------+
        |   EMPLOYEE    |----+
        +---------------+    |  "manages"
             ^               |
             +---------------+        each employee may manage other employees

    BINARY (the everyday case - two boxes, one line):
        +-----------+                      +-----------+
        |   BOOK    |--------------------  | PUBLISHER |
        +-----------+     "published by"   +-----------+

    TERNARY (three boxes meeting at one fact - usually turned into a box, Part 5):
        +----------+        +----------+        +----------+
        |  DOCTOR  |---\    |  DRUG    |    /---| PATIENT  |
        +----------+    \   +----------+   /    +----------+
                         \      |         /
                          \     |        /
                           \ "prescribes to" /
                            (one fact ties all three together)


  Relationships can carry attributes of their OWN. "Member borrows Copy" - but the
  borrow DATE and the due DATE belong to neither the member nor the copy; they
  belong to the borrowing itself. A relationship with its own facts is a strong
  hint that the relationship wants to become an entity (an associative entity -
  here, a "Loan"). Spotting this early saves redesigns later.

  WHOSE FACT IS IT? drawn. The dates fit neither parent; they live on the Loan:
    +---------------------+          +----------------------+
    |      MEMBER         |          |        COPY          |
    | memb_id, name       |          | copy_id, shelf       |
    +---------------------+          +----------------------+
              \                              /
               \  borrowed_on? due_on?      /     <- belong to NEITHER
                \                          /          of these two...
                 v                        v
            +-------------------------------------+
            |               LOAN                  |   ...they belong HERE,
            | loan_id, memb_id, copy_id,          |   to the borrowing itself.
            | borrowed_on, due_on, returned_on    |
            +-------------------------------------+
    +---------+----------+----------+-------------+-------------+-------------+
    | loan_id | memb_id  | copy_id  | borrowed_on | due_on      | returned_on |
    +---------+----------+----------+-------------+-------------+-------------+
    | L-1     | M-1001   | C-2      | 2026-09-30  | 2026-10-14  | (null=out)  |
    | L-2     | M-1002   | C-5      | 2026-09-28  | 2026-10-12  | 2026-10-03  |
    +---------+----------+----------+-------------+-------------+-------------+
    returned_on is NULL on L-1 - its emptiness MEANS "still out" (Part 9).

  >> CONTEMPLATE: "A Student has a Grade in a Course." Whose fact is the grade?
     Not the student alone (they have many), not the course alone (it gives many).
     It belongs to the student-and-course PAIR. Where, then, must it be stored?

  KEY IDEA: entities are nouns, attributes are their adjectives, relationships are
  the verbs - and verbs can have facts of their own.


  2.4  CARDINALITY - the most important question about any relationship
  ---------------------------------------------------------------------
  For every relationship you must answer: how many of each side relate to how
  many of the other? This is CARDINALITY, and it decides the whole table layout.

  Ask it in BOTH directions, one side at a time, in plain English:
    "One member borrows how many copies?"  -> many.
    "One copy is borrowed by how many members (at once)?" -> one.
  That pair of answers ("many" / "one") makes this a ONE-TO-MANY relationship.

  The habit of asking BOTH directions, as a table you can fill in for any link:
  +------------------+--------------------------+--------------------------+------+
  | relationship     | "one LEFT relates to     | "one RIGHT relates to    | type |
  |                  |  how many RIGHT?"        |  how many LEFT?"         |      |
  +------------------+--------------------------+--------------------------+------+
  | Country-Capital  | one                      | one                      | 1:1  |
  | Publisher-Book   | many                     | one                      | 1:N  |
  | Student-Course   | many                     | many                     | M:N  |
  | Member-Loan      | many                     | one                      | 1:N  |
  +------------------+--------------------------+--------------------------+------+
  Fill both middle columns in words, and the last column names itself.

  The three shapes you will meet, with a test question for each:
    - ONE-TO-ONE (1:1). One country has one capital city; one capital belongs to
      one country. Rare, and often a hint the two entities could be one - or are
      split on purpose (optional or sensitive data kept apart).
    - ONE-TO-MANY (1:N). One publisher prints many books; each book has one
      publisher. The workhorse of database design; most links are these.
    - MANY-TO-MANY (M:N). One student takes many courses; one course has many
      students. These CANNOT be stored directly and always need a helper table -
      the single most important pattern in Part 5.


  The three shapes, drawn in crow's foot (how to read the marks is Part 4; for
  now just see the shape). Marks nearest a box = the MAXIMUM on that side:

    ONE-TO-ONE  (1:1):
    +-----------+                                   +-----------+
    |  COUNTRY  |--||===============================||--| CAPITAL|
    +-----------+                                   +-----------+
       exactly one                                 exactly one
       ->  one COUNTRY has exactly one CAPITAL
       <-  one CAPITAL belongs to exactly one COUNTRY

    ONE-TO-MANY  (1:N) - the workhorse:
    +-----------+                                   +-----------+
    | PUBLISHER |--||===============================o<--|  BOOK  |
    +-----------+                                   +-----------+
       exactly one                                 zero or many
       ->  one PUBLISHER publishes zero or many BOOKs
       <-  each BOOK is published by exactly one PUBLISHER

    MANY-TO-MANY  (M:N) - cannot be stored directly, needs Part 5's bridge:
    +-----------+                                   +-----------+
    |  STUDENT  |-->o===============================o<--| COURSE |
    +-----------+                                   +-----------+
       zero or many                                zero or many
       ->  one STUDENT takes zero or many COURSEs
       <-  one COURSE holds zero or many STUDENTs


  PARTICIPATION (the "minimum" side of cardinality)
    Cardinality has a maximum (one/many) AND a minimum (must there be at least
    one, or is zero allowed?). "Must every loan have a book?" - yes, MANDATORY.
    "Must every member have a loan?" - no, a new member has zero, OPTIONAL. The
    minimum is what NULL and "optional" are really about, and it is where a design
    says which situations are allowed to exist at all.

  So each end of a relationship carries a (minimum, maximum) pair. Four little
  symbols in the next part encode exactly these pairs. Get cardinality right and
  the diagram almost draws itself; get it wrong and no amount of later cleverness
  saves the design.

  The (min, max) pair for every end, laid out so you can see all four at once:
  +----------+----------+----------------+---------------------------------------+
  | minimum  | maximum  | plain words    | real example (an end of a link)       |
  +----------+----------+----------------+---------------------------------------+
  | 0 (opt)  | 1        | zero or one    | a person's optional spouse record     |
  | 1 (mand) | 1        | exactly one    | a loan's book (must have one)         |
  | 0 (opt)  | many     | zero or many   | a member's loans (new member has 0)   |
  | 1 (mand) | many     | one or many    | an invoice's lines (at least one)     |
  +----------+----------+----------------+---------------------------------------+

  >> CONTEMPLATE: for your coffee-shop loyalty system - one card belongs to how
     many customers? one customer holds how many cards? Is a customer allowed to
     exist with zero cards? Your answers ARE the design of that link.

  TRY IT: write the (min, max) pair for BOTH ends of "a department employs
     staff." Must a department have at least one employee? Can a brand-new,
     not-yet-staffed department exist? Can an employee belong to no department?
     Your three answers decide whether the FK may ever be NULL (Part 3).


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  PART 3 - KEYS: HOW A DATABASE KNOWS "WHICH ONE"
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Two rows that look alike are a disaster waiting to happen. Keys are how a database
tells things apart and how one table points reliably at a row in another. Identity
is the quiet foundation everything else stands on.

  CANDIDATE KEYS - any attribute (or set of attributes) whose value is unique for
  every row and never empty could serve to identify the row. A member might have
  several: their membership number, and perhaps their email. Each is a CANDIDATE.

  Drawn: in this member table, THREE columns are each unique and never empty -
  so there are three candidate keys. Any one COULD be the identifier:
    +----------+-------------+---------------------+-------------+
    | memb_id  | memb_no     | email               | name        |
    +----------+-------------+---------------------+-------------+
    | M-1001   | 100244      | sara@example.org    | Sara Adib   |
    | M-1002   | 100245      | omar@example.org    | Omar Reyes  |
    | M-1003   | 100246      | lee@example.org     | Mina Lee    |
    +----------+-------------+---------------------+-------------+
       ^candidate   ^candidate      ^candidate          not unique
       (surrogate)  (natural)       (natural, but        (two people
                                     changes!)            can share a name)
    name is NOT a candidate: two members could be "Sara Adib." The other three
    each uniquely pick one row - they are the candidates we choose a PK from.

  PRIMARY KEY - you pick ONE candidate to be the official identifier. Every table
  gets exactly one primary key; it is unique, never NULL, and ideally never
  changes. Everything that points at this row will point using this value.

  WHAT MAKES A GOOD PRIMARY KEY? It should be:
    - UNIQUE (by definition), and guaranteed so, not just "probably".
    - STABLE - it should never need to change. (This rules out a lot of tempting
      choices, see below.)
    - MINIMAL and MEANINGLESS is often best - the less real-world baggage it
      carries, the less reason it will ever have to change.


  Score the tempting candidates against the three rules - this is why we lean to a
  made-up id:
  +---------------+--------+--------+-----------+--------------------------------+
  | candidate     | unique?| stable?| meaning?  | verdict as primary key         |
  +---------------+--------+--------+-----------+--------------------------------+
  | name          | no     | no     | yes       | never (repeats, changes)       |
  | email         | yes-ish| no     | yes       | risky (people change email)    |
  | memb_no       | yes    | mostly | yes       | ok natural key; keep UNIQUE    |
  | made-up id    | yes    | YES    | no        | best PK; add a unique natural  |
  | (surrogate)   |        |        |           | column for humans              |
  +---------------+--------+--------+-----------+--------------------------------+

  >> CONTEMPLATE: should a person's name be the primary key? Names repeat (two
     John Smiths), names change (marriage), names have no guaranteed format. Every
     property of a good key is violated. So - almost never. What about email?
     Unique-ish, but people change emails, and then everything pointing at them
     breaks. This tension leads straight to the next idea.

  NATURAL vs SURROGATE KEYS - a real design fork
    A NATURAL key is a real-world value that already identifies the thing: an
    ISBN for a book, a country code, a membership number printed on the card.
    A SURROGATE key is a meaningless value the system invents purely to identify
    the row: an auto-incrementing number (1, 2, 3...) or a random UUID like
    3f2a...-.... It means nothing outside the database.

    Both an auto-number AND a UUID are surrogate keys - that is not a
    contradiction. "Surrogate" means "invented, meaningless"; it does not mean
    "a number." The two just make the meaningless id in different ways:
    +----------------+-------------------------+----------------------------------+
    | surrogate kind | looks like              | trade-off                        |
    +----------------+-------------------------+----------------------------------+
    | auto-number    | 1, 2, 3, 4 ...          | tiny, readable; but reveals row  |
    |                |                         | counts and collides if you merge |
    |                |                         | two databases                    |
    | UUID           | 3f2a9c7e-...-b1          | bulky, ugly; but unique across   |
    |                |                         | every machine, leaks nothing,    |
    |                |                         | safe to generate anywhere        |
    +----------------+-------------------------+----------------------------------+


    Trade-offs:
      - Natural keys need no extra column and read meaningfully, BUT they change
        (and then every reference must change too), and "guaranteed unique
        forever" is rarer than it looks.
      - Surrogate keys never change and are always clean, BUT they carry no
        meaning, so you still keep the natural value as an ordinary (indexed,
        unique) column for humans to search by.
    Most modern designs use a surrogate primary key (auto-number or UUID) AND keep
    the natural identifier as a separate UNIQUE column - best of both. student-panel
    does exactly this: UUID primary keys, plus a human "student_code" kept unique.

    THE MODERN DEFAULT, DRAWN (student-panel's own pattern): a UUID primary key
    PLUS a human-readable student_code under a UNIQUE constraint:
    +----------------------------+---------------+----------------+
    | id (UUID, PK)              | student_code  | full_name      |
    |                            | (natural,     |                |
    |                            |  UNIQUE)      |                |
    +----------------------------+---------------+----------------+
    | 3f2a9c7e-6b10-4a....-b1    | STU-0001      | Sara Adib      |
    | 9c01d4e2-11af-4d....-7e    | STU-0002      | Omar Reyes     |
    | a7bb30f9-2c84-4f....-02    | STU-0003      | Mina Lee       |
    +----------------------------+---------------+----------------+
    The id never changes and other tables point at IT. The student_code is what
    staff type and read; UNIQUE stops two students sharing a code. Best of both.
    (In YOUR practice designs you may use a plain auto-number id instead of a UUID
    - simpler to write by hand on paper. Same idea, same role; just smaller.)

  COMPOSITE KEYS - sometimes identity takes more than one column. In a table that
  records "student X is a member of group Y", the pair (student, group) together
  is the identity - neither alone is unique. A key made of several columns is a
  COMPOSITE key, and it is completely normal, especially in the link tables of
  Part 5.


  Drawn: the pair (student_id, group_id) together is the key. Look - no single
  column is unique, but no PAIR repeats:
    +------------+-----------+--------------+
    | student_id | group_id  | joined_on    |     PK = (student_id, group_id)
    +------------+-----------+--------------+
    | STU-0001   | G-ART     | 2026-09-01   |     STU-0001 appears twice - fine
    | STU-0001   | G-MATH    | 2026-09-01   |     G-ART appears twice   - fine
    | STU-0002   | G-ART     | 2026-09-02   |     but (STU-0001,G-ART) only once
    +------------+-----------+--------------+
    Trying to add (STU-0001, G-ART) again is refused by the composite key - you
    cannot enrol the same student in the same group twice. The key encodes a rule.

  >> CONTEMPLATE: UUID vs a simple counting number for a primary key. The counter
     is smaller and human-readable but reveals how many rows exist and collides if
     you ever merge two databases. A UUID is bulky and ugly but unique across every
     machine on earth and leaks nothing. Which fits a small library? Which fits an
     app that might sync between devices? (This is a real student-panel choice.)

  FOREIGN KEYS - how a relationship becomes real
    A relationship in your head becomes, in tables, a FOREIGN KEY: a column in one
    table that holds the primary-key value of a row in another table. The Loan
    table has a book_id column whose value is the id of some row in Book. That
    column is a foreign key; it POINTS. Following it is how the database re-joins
    what your design split apart.

    Drawn: a book carries its publisher's id. The arrow is the pointer:
    PUBLISHER (the "one")                 BOOK (the "many", holds the FK)
    +---------+-------------+             +--------+-----------+--------------+
    | pub_id  | name        |             | book_id| title     | pub_id (FK)  |
    +---------+-------------+             +--------+-----------+--------------+
    | PUB-1   | Chatto      |<--------+   | B-1    | Dune      | PUB-2        |
    | PUB-2   | Gollancz    |<-----+  +---| B-2    | Emma      | PUB-1        |
    +---------+-------------+      |      | B-3    | The Hobbit| PUB-2        |
                                   +------+--------+-----------+--------------+
    B-1's pub_id = PUB-2, so "Dune is published by Gollancz." B-3 points at PUB-2
    too. The publisher's name is stored ONCE (in PUBLISHER) and pointed at from
    many books - exactly the "each fact in one place" rule, made real by a key.


    REFERENTIAL INTEGRITY is the promise the database enforces: a foreign key must
    point at a row that actually exists (or be empty, if the link is optional). It
    will refuse to store a loan for book #9999 if no such book exists, and it will
    stop you deleting a book that still has loans - unless you tell it what should
    happen instead. Those "what happens on delete" rules (cascade the delete,
    set the link empty, or forbid the delete) are themselves design decisions
    about what the world allows. A foreign key left pointing at a row that no
    longer exists is an ORPHAN - referential integrity exists precisely to make
    orphans impossible.

    THE "WHAT HAPPENS WHEN THE PARENT IS DELETED" RULES, as a plain sentence each.
    Say the sentence aloud and pick the one that is TRUE of your world:
    +----------------+------------------------------+---------------------------+
    | ON DELETE rule | what happens to the children | the rule as a sentence    |
    +----------------+------------------------------+---------------------------+
    | RESTRICT       | the delete is refused while  | "you may not delete a book|
    | (the default   | any child still points here | while copies of it exist" |
    |  you want most)|                              |                           |
    | CASCADE        | the children are deleted too | "deleting an order deletes|
    |                |                              | its order-lines with it"  |
    | SET NULL       | the child survives; its FK   | "if a category is deleted,|
    |                | becomes NULL (link cleared)  | its products become       |
    |                | (FK must be allowed NULL)    | uncategorised, not lost"  |
    +----------------+------------------------------+---------------------------+
    The rule as one plain sentence: a foreign key must always point at a real row
    or at nothing - never at a ghost. RESTRICT/CASCADE/SET NULL are just the three
    honest answers to "...so what do we do when the real row goes away?"

  WHERE DOES THE FOREIGN KEY GO? A rule you can lean on:
    - In a ONE-TO-MANY, the key goes on the MANY side. Each book has one publisher,
      so book carries publisher_id - not the other way round. (Put it on the "one"
      side and you would need many values in one cell, which we forbade.)
    - In a ONE-TO-ONE, it can go on either side; put it where participation is
      optional, or where it reads most naturally.
    - In a MANY-TO-MANY, neither side can hold it - which forces the helper table
      of Part 5, and the foreign keys live THERE.


    The FK lives on the many side - drawn, with the crow's foot sitting exactly
    where the FK lives:
    +-----------+                                   +-------------------+
    | PUBLISHER |--||===============================o<--| BOOK           |
    +-----------+                                   | ...               |
                                                    | pub_id  <- FK HERE |
                                                    +-------------------+
       the "one" side                               the "many" side holds the FK
       (no FK here)                                 and wears the crow's foot
    Memorise the pairing: crow's foot = many side = where the FK column lives =
    the child pointing at its parent.

  KEY IDEA: a primary key is a row's name; a foreign key is another row saying
  that name to point at it. Relationships are just keys pointing at keys.

  TRY IT: in the Member/Loan/Copy picture from Part 2.3, write which table holds
     which foreign key, and for each pick an ON DELETE rule and say it aloud. (Hint:
     Loan is the many side of both links. Should deleting a member delete their
     loan history, or be refused? There is no single right answer - say your
     reason. That is Part 9 thinking, arriving early.)


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  PART 4 - READING AND DRAWING ER DIAGRAMS
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A picture lets you see a whole design at once and spot what is wrong. There are a
couple of notations; we use the CROW'S FOOT style, because it is the most common
today and it is what your student-panel diagram uses.

  THE PIECES
    - Each ENTITY is a box. Inside, one line per attribute (many tools also tag
      the key attributes). The box is the table.
    - A RELATIONSHIP is a LINE between two boxes.
    - The ENDS of the line carry the cardinality - read each end as the (minimum,
      maximum) pair from Part 2.4.

  THE THREE GLYPHS - this is the whole alphabet:
  +--------+-------------------+-------------------------------------------------+
  | glyph  | means             | note                                            |
  +--------+-------------------+-------------------------------------------------+
  |   |    | ONE               | a single bar                                    |
  |   o    | ZERO / optional   | a small circle                                  |
  |   <    | MANY              | the "crow's foot" (also drawn > on a left end)  |
  +--------+-------------------+-------------------------------------------------+

  THE ONE RULE FOR READING THE MARKS. Each end of a line has TWO marks. Read them
  OUTSIDE-IN, toward the box:
    - the FAR mark (further from the box, toward the line's middle) = the MINIMUM.
          o = optional (minimum zero)      | = mandatory (minimum one)
    - the NEAR mark (right against the box) = the MAXIMUM.
          | = one (maximum one)            < = many (maximum many)
  So "near tells you one-or-many; far tells you optional-or-mandatory."


  THE FOUR END-COMBINATIONS. Here written for an entity on the RIGHT of the line
  (so the order left-to-right is far-mark then near-mark, i.e. MIN then MAX):
  +----------------+----------------+-------------------------------------------+
  | right-end mark | reads as       | meaning (min, max)                        |
  +----------------+----------------+-------------------------------------------+
  |   ---o|  BOX   | zero or one    | optional, at most one   (0,1)             |
  |   ---||  BOX   | exactly one    | mandatory, exactly one  (1,1)             |
  |   ---o<  BOX   | zero or many   | optional, many          (0,many)          |
  |   ---|<  BOX   | one or many    | mandatory, at least one (1,many)          |
  +----------------+----------------+-------------------------------------------+

  THE MIRROR. The marks flip left-to-right when the entity is on the LEFT of the
  line - the crow's foot points the other way and the pair reverses, but the
  MEANING is identical. "o< on a right end" is the same as ">o on a left end":
  +----------------+------------------+----------------------------------------+
  | left-end mark  | right-end mirror | both mean                              |
  +----------------+------------------+----------------------------------------+
  |  BOX  |o---     |     ---o|  BOX   | zero or one                            |
  |  BOX  ||---     |     ---||  BOX   | exactly one                            |
  |  BOX  >o---     |     ---o<  BOX   | zero or many                           |
  |  BOX  >|---     |     ---|<  BOX   | one or many                            |
  +----------------+------------------+----------------------------------------+
  Do not let the flip worry you: pick the box, find its two marks, read near=max
  and far=min. It says the same thing from either side.

  READING A LINE
    You read a crow's-foot line from each side toward the other. For
       Publisher ||--o< Book
    read left-to-right: "one publisher has zero-or-many books," and right-to-left:
    "each book has exactly one publisher." Both sentences must be TRUE of the real
    world - saying them aloud is the best test of your own diagram.


  THE COMMON SHAPES, DRAWN (compact inline AND as boxes - learn both forms):
    1:1   Country ||--|| Capital        one and only one each way
    1:N   Publisher ||--o< Book          one publisher, many books
    M:N   Student >o--o< Course          many each way - needs a helper (Part 5)

    optional 1:N worth staring at - a member may have NO loans (o near Loan's
    "many" is really about the member end), yet every loan MUST have exactly one
    copy. Drawn as boxes:
    +-----------+                               +-----------+
    |  MEMBER   |--||===========================o<--|  LOAN  |
    +-----------+                               +-----------+
       exactly one member                       zero or many loans
       ->  one MEMBER has zero or many LOANs   (a brand-new member has zero)
       <-  each LOAN belongs to exactly one MEMBER (never a loan with no member)

    +-----------+                               +-----------+
    |   LOAN    |--||===========================||--|  COPY  |
    +-----------+                               +-----------+
       ->  each LOAN is of exactly one COPY
       <-  each COPY is on exactly one LOAN AT A TIME (over time, many - see note)
    The little circle vs bar near the box is where "is this allowed to be empty?"
    lives. Change one circle to a bar and you have changed what the world permits.

  A TINY WORKED DIAGRAM (in text)
    Author ||--o< Book           one author writes many books; each book, one author
    Book   ||--o< Copy           a title has many physical copies; each copy, one title
    Read those four sentences aloud. If any is false for a real library (maybe a
    book has several authors?), the diagram is wrong and must change - better to
    find it now, on paper, than after building. (A book with several authors is
    itself a many-to-many - and you now know what that needs. Part 5.)


  A WHOLE SMALL MODEL AT A GLANCE (preview of Part 8's library; full tables there).
  See how the boxes and marks let you take in an entire design in one look:

    +----------+          +----------+          +----------+
    |  AUTHOR  |--||====o<--|  BOOK  |--||====o<--|  COPY  |
    +----------+          +----------+          +----------+
      one author             one book               each copy belongs to one
      many books             many copies             book; has many loans over
                                                      time...  |
                                                               o<
                                                               |
    +----------+                                        +--------------+
    |  MEMBER  |--||==================================o<--|   LOAN      |
    +----------+                                        +--------------+
      one member has zero-or-many loans; each loan has exactly one member AND
      exactly one copy. LOAN sits at the "many" end of two links - it is a bridge.

  ANOTHER NOTATION YOU'LL SEE (Chen)
    The older Chen notation draws relationships as DIAMONDS between the boxes, with
    numbers (1, N, M) on the lines, and attributes as ovals. It is common in
    textbooks. You don't need to draw it, only to recognise it: a diamond labelled
    "borrows" between Member and Copy is the same idea as a crow's-foot line.

      +----------+        / \         +----------+
      |  MEMBER  |---1---<   >---N---| COPY     |
      +----------+        \ /         +----------+
                       borrows
    "1 ... N" on the diamond's arms = one member borrows N copies. Same meaning as
    Member ||--o< Copy in crow's foot. Different picture, identical idea.


  TRY IT: on your blank sheet, draw Customer, Order, and Product as three boxes.
    A customer places many orders; each order belongs to one customer. An order
    contains many products, and a product appears on many orders. Draw the lines,
    put the right symbols on the ends, and say all four sentences aloud. You will
    find one line you cannot draw cleanly - hold that feeling; Part 5 is its cure.

  WHAT YOU PROBABLY DREW (and the line that fought back):
    +------------+                           +-----------+
    |  CUSTOMER  |--||=======================o<--|  ORDER  |      clean 1:N
    +------------+                           +-----------+
       one customer                          zero or many orders

    +-----------+                            +-----------+
    |   ORDER   |-->o=======================o<--| PRODUCT |      M:N - won't go!
    +-----------+                            +-----------+
       many each way: one order has many products, one product on many orders
    The Customer-Order line is a tidy 1:N. The Order-Product line is MANY-TO-MANY,
    and there is nowhere to hang a foreign key - that is the feeling to hold.
    Part 5.1 names the fix (it is the order LINE), and you will have met it twice
    before you need it.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  PART 5 - THE PATTERNS BEGINNERS TRIP ON
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Everything so far handles the easy links. A handful of situations need a specific
move each. Learn these five and very little in real design will surprise you.
Here they are on one card, with the single move each one needs:
  +---+----------------------------+--------------------------------------------+
  | # | situation                  | the one move                               |
  +---+----------------------------+--------------------------------------------+
  | 1 | many-to-many               | a BRIDGE table (one row per pairing)       |
  | 2 | relationship w/ own facts  | that bridge/table HOLDS the facts          |
  | 3 | one-to-one                 | usually MERGE; split only on purpose       |
  | 4 | self-reference (a tree)    | a parent_id FK into the SAME table         |
  | 5 | ternary (three-way fact)   | a table in the MIDDLE with three FKs       |
  +---+----------------------------+--------------------------------------------+
  Notice how often the move is the same shape: a table in the middle holding
  foreign keys. Learn that one move and most "hard" designs become routine.

  5.1  MANY-TO-MANY NEEDS A BRIDGE TABLE  (the big one)
  ----------------------------------------------------
  A student takes many courses; a course holds many students. Try to store this
  with a foreign key and you fail both ways: you cannot list many course-ids in a
  student's single cell, nor many student-ids in a course's. A table cell holds
  one value - so a direct many-to-many is impossible.

  THE IMPOSSIBLE ATTEMPT, drawn - a comma-list in a cell (breaks 1NF, Part 6):
    +------------+-------------+----------------------------+
    | student_id | name        | courses (DO NOT DO THIS)   |
    +------------+-------------+----------------------------+
    | STU-0001   | Sara Adib   | ART101, MATH210, HIST105   |  <- many in one cell
    | STU-0002   | Omar Reyes  | MATH210                    |
    +------------+-------------+----------------------------+
    You cannot ask "who is in MATH210?" without picking strings apart; you cannot
    store a grade per course; adding a course means editing text. Dead end.


  The cure: invent a THIRD table that holds one row per pairing. Call it Enrolment.
  Each row has one student_id and one course_id - two foreign keys - and together
  they identify the row (a composite key, Part 3). Now:
       Student ||--o< Enrolment >o--|| Course
  The impossible M:N has become two easy 1:N links through a table in the middle.
  This helper is called a BRIDGE, JUNCTION, LINK, or ASSOCIATIVE table - same thing.

  THE CURE, drawn as boxes - two tidy 1:N links meeting in the middle:
    +-----------+                 +---------------+                 +----------+
    |  STUDENT  |--||=========o<--| ENROLMENT     |--o<=========||--| COURSE   |
    +-----------+                 | student_id FK |                 +----------+
    one student has              | course_id  FK |              one course has
    many enrolments              | grade         |              many enrolments
                                 +---------------+
       ->  one STUDENT has many ENROLMENTs; each ENROLMENT is for one STUDENT
       <-  one COURSE has many ENROLMENTs; each ENROLMENT is for one COURSE

  THE CURE, drawn as rows that connect (keep the ids consistent and follow them):
    STUDENT                         COURSE
    +----------+-----------+        +-----------+------------------+
    | stud_id  | name      |        | course_id | title            |
    +----------+-----------+        +-----------+------------------+
    | STU-0001 | Sara Adib |        | ART101    | Drawing          |
    | STU-0002 | Omar Reyes|        | MATH210   | Calculus         |
    +----------+-----------+        | HIST105   | Modern History   |
                                    +-----------+------------------+
    ENROLMENT  (the bridge; PK = (stud_id, course_id))
    +----------+-----------+------------+--------+
    | stud_id  | course_id | enrolled_on| grade  |
    +----------+-----------+------------+--------+
    | STU-0001 | ART101    | 2026-09-01 | A      |   Sara is in Drawing...
    | STU-0001 | MATH210   | 2026-09-01 | B      |   ...and Calculus...
    | STU-0001 | HIST105   | 2026-09-02 | (null) |
    | STU-0002 | MATH210   | 2026-09-01 | A      |   Omar is in Calculus
    +----------+-----------+------------+--------+
    Now "who is in MATH210?" = enrolment rows with course_id=MATH210 -> Sara,Omar.
    "Sara's courses?" = rows with stud_id=STU-0001 -> Drawing, Calculus, History.


  THE BONUS: this bridge is the natural home for facts about the PAIRING itself -
  the enrolment date, the final grade, whether it was dropped. Remember from Part
  2.3 that a relationship can have its own attributes; the bridge is WHERE they
  live. When a relationship earns its own facts, it has quietly become an entity.
  Above, enrolled_on and grade belong to the student-and-course PAIR - nowhere else.

  COMPOSITE PK vs OWN SURROGATE id - a choice you must make for every bridge.
    Two honest options; pick one and WRITE DOWN which:
    +----------------------------+---------------------------------------------+
    | option                     | consequence                                 |
    +----------------------------+---------------------------------------------+
    | PK = (stud_id, course_id)  | the pair IS the identity; no two rows can    |
    | (composite, no extra id)   | repeat a pairing - the rule is free          |
    | PK = enrolment_id          | a tidy single id to point at from elsewhere, |
    | (own surrogate) + UNIQUE   | BUT you MUST add UNIQUE(stud_id, course_id)  |
    | (stud_id, course_id)       | or duplicates sneak back in                  |
    +----------------------------+---------------------------------------------+
    The rule to say every time: if a bridge gets its own surrogate id, decide
    whether the natural combination should carry a UNIQUE constraint - and here it
    must, because "the same student in the same course twice" is nonsense. (This
    is exactly how student-panel's scores table behaves: it bridges a student and
    a parameter for a period, and the combination is kept unique.)

  >> CONTEMPLATE: your Customer-Order-Product sketch from Part 4. Order-to-Product
     is many-to-many. What is the bridge? (It is the order LINE: one row per "this
     order, this product, this quantity, this price".) Notice the bridge holds
     facts - quantity, price - that belong to neither the order nor the product
     alone. That is the tell that it deserved to exist.


  5.2  ONE-TO-ONE: usually a question, sometimes a choice
  -------------------------------------------------------
  A true 1:1 is uncommon. When you find one, ask first: should these be one
  entity? A Person and their Passport-details might just be more columns on Person.
  But 1:1 splits are made ON PURPOSE when: the extra data is rarely present (keep
  it out of the main table so most rows stay lean), or it is sensitive and kept
  separate for access control, or it is huge (a profile photo) and you don't want
  to drag it along every time you read the main row. So 1:1 = "merge, unless you
  have a reason to split" - and know your reason.

  MERGED (the usual answer) - one table, passport columns may be NULL:
    +----------+-------------+---------------+-----------------+
    | person_id| name        | passport_no   | passport_expiry |
    +----------+-------------+---------------+-----------------+
    | P-1      | Sara Adib   | 981222345     | 2030-05-01      |
    | P-2      | Omar Reyes  | (null)        | (null)          |   no passport
    +----------+-------------+---------------+-----------------+

  SPLIT ON PURPOSE - sensitive/rare data in its own table; the FK goes on the
  never-empty side (passport) with UNIQUE + NOT NULL, so it stays truly 1:1:
    PERSON                              PASSPORT (zero-or-one per person)
    +----------+-------------+          +-----------+-----------+-------------+
    | person_id| name        |          | person_id | number    | expiry      |
    +----------+-------------+          | (FK,UNIQUE|           |             |
    | P-1      | Sara Adib   |<---------|  NOT NULL)|           |             |
    | P-2      | Omar Reyes  |          +-----------+-----------+-------------+
    +----------+-------------+          | P-1       | 981222345 | 2030-05-01  |
                                        +-----------+-----------+-------------+
    person_id in PASSPORT is UNIQUE (so one person cannot have two passport rows)
    and NOT NULL (so a passport row cannot float free of a person). Those two
    constraints are what MAKE it one-to-one rather than one-to-many. P-2 simply
    has no row here - that is the "zero" in "zero or one."

    Drawn as a line:
    +----------+                               +------------+
    |  PERSON  |--|o===========================||--| PASSPORT |
    +----------+                               +------------+
       ->  one PERSON has zero or one PASSPORT
       <-  each PASSPORT belongs to exactly one PERSON


  5.3  SELF-REFERENCING RELATIONSHIPS: trees and hierarchies
  ----------------------------------------------------------
  Sometimes an entity relates to OTHERS OF ITS OWN KIND. An employee has a manager
  who is another employee. A category has a parent category. A comment replies to
  another comment. You model this with a foreign key that points back into the
  SAME table: employee.manager_id points at another employee.id.

  Drawn as rows - manager_id points at another row's id in the SAME table; the
  boss has manager_id = NULL (the root of the tree):
    +--------+---------------+--------------+
    | emp_id | name          | manager_id   |
    +--------+---------------+--------------+
    | E-1    | Dana (CEO)    | (null)       |   <- root: reports to no one
    | E-2    | Raj           | E-1          |   Raj reports to Dana
    | E-3    | Mina          | E-1          |   Mina reports to Dana
    | E-4    | Sol           | E-2          |   Sol reports to Raj
    | E-5    | Yara          | E-2          |   Yara reports to Raj
    +--------+---------------+--------------+

  The same rows, drawn as the TREE they encode:
            E-1 Dana (CEO)            manager_id = NULL
            /          \
         E-2 Raj      E-3 Mina        manager_id = E-1
         /     \
      E-4 Sol  E-5 Yara               manager_id = E-2

  Drawn as a line that loops back to the same box:
    +----------------+
    |   EMPLOYEE     |----+
    |  emp_id        |    |  "is managed by"
    |  manager_id FK |    |   o| (zero or one manager: the root has none)
    +----------------+    |   |<  on the other arm (a manager has one-or-many
            ^             |        reports) - same box, both ends
            +-------------+

  This builds a TREE: a top row whose parent is empty (NULL - the root), rows
  beneath it, rows beneath those, to any depth. It is how you store "folders inside
  folders" shapes with no fixed number of levels. student-panel uses this twice -
  student groups can nest inside groups, and parameters inside parameters - so it
  is worth getting comfortable with now. (student-panel also PREVENTS CYCLES: a
  group may not end up being its own ancestor - a rule the self-reference alone
  does not enforce, so the app checks it.)


  >> CONTEMPLATE: why can't you just make columns parent, grandparent,
     great-grandparent? Because you would have to guess the maximum depth forever,
     and most rows would leave them empty. A single self-pointing parent_id handles
     ANY depth with one column. Recursion beats a fixed guess. (Asking "give me the
     whole branch under this node" then needs a special looping query - a real cost
     of this elegant trick, and a fair thing to weigh.)

  >> PITFALL: forgetting the root. SOMETHING has to sit at the top with no parent.
     If every row insists on a non-NULL parent_id, you can never insert the first
     row (nothing exists yet to point at) - an insert anomaly born of a too-strict
     rule. Let the root's parent_id be NULL, and give that NULL the plain meaning
     "this is a top-level node."

  5.4  WEAK / DEPENDENT ENTITIES
  ------------------------------
  Some things cannot exist on their own. An order-line has no meaning without its
  order; a room has no meaning without its building. These are WEAK entities: they
  depend on a "parent" (the strong entity) for their very existence, and often for
  part of their identity too (order-line #3 only makes sense as "line 3 OF order
  #88"). In practice you model a weak entity as its own table with a mandatory
  foreign key to its parent, and usually an ON-DELETE rule that removes the
  children when the parent goes (the lines die with their order). The design
  question a weak entity answers is "can this ever stand alone?" - and the answer
  shapes both its key and its delete behaviour.

  Drawn - an order and its lines (note: the table is "orders", never "order" -
  "order" is a reserved word in SQL; see Part 9):
    ORDERS (strong)                 ORDER_LINE (weak; dies with its order)
    +----------+------------+       +----------+---------+----------+-----+
    | order_id | placed_on  |       | order_id | line_no | product  | qty |
    +----------+------------+       | (FK, PK  |         |          |     |
    | O-88     | 2026-09-30 |<------|  part)   |         |          |     |
    | O-89     | 2026-10-01 |<--+   +----------+---------+----------+-----+
    +----------+------------+   |   | O-88     | 1       | Pencils  | 3   |
                                |   | O-88     | 2       | Eraser   | 1   |
                                +---| O-89     | 1       | Notebook | 2   |
                                    +----------+---------+----------+-----+
    PK of ORDER_LINE = (order_id, line_no): "line 1 of O-88" - line_no alone means
    nothing. order_id is mandatory (NOT NULL): a line with no order is impossible.
    ON DELETE CASCADE: delete O-88 and its two lines go with it (they cannot
    outlive it). That is the weak entity's whole character in one picture.


  5.5  TERNARY RELATIONSHIPS: prefer an entity in the middle
  ----------------------------------------------------------
  "A supplier supplies a part to a project" links three entities in one fact. You
  CAN draw a three-way relationship, but it is hard to read and harder to add facts
  to. The usual move is the same as for many-to-many: create an entity in the
  middle - a "Supply" row with three foreign keys (supplier, part, project) plus
  its own facts (quantity, price, date). Almost every messy multi-way relationship
  becomes clean once you let the relationship itself become a table.

  Drawn - three parents point INTO one middle table; the fact lives in the middle:
      +-----------+        +-----------+        +-----------+
      | SUPPLIER  |        |   PART    |        | PROJECT   |
      +-----------+        +-----------+        +-----------+
            |                   |                    |
            | ||                | ||                 | ||
            o<                  o<                   o<
            |                   |                    |
            +---------------+   |    +---------------+
                            v   v    v
                   +-----------------------------+
                   |           SUPPLY            |
                   | supplier_id FK              |
                   | part_id     FK              |
                   | project_id  FK              |
                   | quantity, unit_price, date  |
                   +-----------------------------+

  As rows (the three FKs plus the pairing's own facts):
    +------------+--------+------------+-----+-----------+------------+
    | supplier_id| part_id| project_id | qty | unit_price| supplied_on|
    +------------+--------+------------+-----+-----------+------------+
    | SUP-1      | P-BOLT | PRJ-BRIDGE | 500 | 0.12      | 2026-09-10 |
    | SUP-1      | P-BOLT | PRJ-TOWER  | 200 | 0.12      | 2026-09-11 |
    | SUP-2      | P-NUT  | PRJ-BRIDGE | 500 | 0.08      | 2026-09-12 |
    +------------+--------+------------+-----+-----------+------------+
    If SUPPLY gets its own surrogate supply_id, decide whether the natural TRIPLE
    (supplier_id, part_id, project_id) should be UNIQUE. Here: can the same
    supplier supply the same part to the same project on two dates? If yes, the
    triple is NOT unique by itself and the date is part of what distinguishes rows;
    if no, add UNIQUE on the triple. Say which, and why - that is the design.

  KEY IDEA: whenever a link is many-to-many, self-referencing, or carries its own
  facts, the answer is almost always the same shape - a table in the middle whose
  rows are the pairings (or triplings), holding foreign keys and any facts of their
  own. Learn that one move and most "hard" designs become routine.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  PART 6 - NORMALISATION: PUTTING EACH FACT IN ONE PLACE
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Normalisation sounds intimidating and is actually one idea repeated: organise
your tables so that each fact is stored exactly once. Everything below is that
idea, made progressively stricter.

  THE DISEASE: redundancy and its "anomalies"
    Picture one wide table, one row per loan, repeating the member's name, phone,
    and address on every single loan they ever make. Here it is with enough rows
    to feel the trouble:

    +---------+-------------+--------------+------------+------------+
    | loan_id | member_name | member_phone | book_title | due_date   |
    +---------+-------------+--------------+------------+------------+
    | 1       | Sara Adib   | 555-1020     | Dune       | 2026-10-14 |
    | 2       | Sara Adib   | 555-1020     | Emma       | 2026-10-14 |
    | 3       | Omar Reyes  | 555-7788     | Dune       | 2026-10-20 |
    | 4       | Sara Adib   | 555-1020     | The Hobbit | 2026-10-28 |
    +---------+-------------+--------------+------------+------------+
    Sara's name and phone are written three times. That triple-storage is the
    disease; the three "anomalies" below are its symptoms.


    Three illnesses follow, and they are worth naming because you will feel them.
    Each is drawn as the exact bad moment it causes:

    UPDATE ANOMALY - Sara changes her phone; you fix some rows and miss one:
    +---------+-------------+--------------+------------+
    | loan_id | member_name | member_phone | book_title |
    +---------+-------------+--------------+------------+
    | 1       | Sara Adib   | 555-2000 (new)| Dune      |
    | 2       | Sara Adib   | 555-2000 (new)| Emma      |
    | 4       | Sara Adib   | 555-1020 (OLD!)| The Hobbit|  <- missed
    +---------+-------------+--------------+------------+
    The table now claims Sara has two phone numbers and cannot say which is true.

    INSERT ANOMALY - a new member, Mina, who has not borrowed anything yet:
    +---------+-------------+--------------+------------+
    | loan_id | member_name | member_phone | book_title |
    +---------+-------------+--------------+------------+
    | ???     | Mina Lee    | 555-3030     | (null?)    |  <- no loan to attach to
    +---------+-------------+--------------+------------+
    There is nowhere to record Mina. The table is ABOUT loans, so a member with no
    loan has no home. Her existence wrongly depends on her having borrowed a book.

    DELETE ANOMALY - Omar returns his only book and you delete that one loan:
    +---------+-------------+--------------+------------+
    | loan_id | member_name | member_phone | book_title |
    +---------+-------------+--------------+------------+
    | 3       | Omar Reyes  | 555-7788     | Dune       |  <- delete this row...
    +---------+-------------+--------------+------------+
    ...and Omar vanishes entirely - his name and phone existed ONLY on that row.
    You meant to erase a loan; you erased a person.

    All three come from ONE mistake: two different KINDS of fact (facts about the
    member, facts about the loan) crammed into one table. The cure is to split
    them so each kind of fact lives in its own table, linked by a key.


  THE CURE, IN STEPS (the "normal forms")
    Each form fixes one kind of muddle. You mostly aim for the third.

    FIRST NORMAL FORM (1NF) - atomic values, no repeating groups.
      Every cell holds ONE value, and no row has "columns" like phone1, phone2,
      phone3. If a thing has many values, it belongs in its own rows/table (the
      multi-valued attribute of Part 2.2, and the bridge of Part 5). Tidy cells.

      NOT 1NF (repeating group of phone columns; most cells empty, max guessed):
      +----------+-------------+----------+----------+----------+
      | memb_id  | name        | phone1   | phone2   | phone3   |
      +----------+-------------+----------+----------+----------+
      | M-1001   | Sara Adib   | 555-1020 | 555-3344 | (null)   |
      | M-1002   | Omar Reyes  | 555-7788 | (null)   | (null)   |
      +----------+-------------+----------+----------+----------+
      1NF (one value per cell; many phones become many ROWS in their own table):
      MEMBER                          MEMBER_PHONE
      +----------+-------------+      +----------+----------+
      | memb_id  | name        |      | memb_id  | number   |
      +----------+-------------+      +----------+----------+
      | M-1001   | Sara Adib   |      | M-1001   | 555-1020 |
      | M-1002   | Omar Reyes  |      | M-1001   | 555-3344 |
      +----------+-------------+      | M-1002   | 555-7788 |
                                      +----------+----------+

    SECOND NORMAL FORM (2NF) - no partial dependency on part of a composite key.
      Only bites when your key is composite. In an Enrolment keyed by (student,
      course), the student's NAME depends on the student alone - only part of the
      key - so it does not belong here; it belongs in Student. Every non-key fact
      must depend on the WHOLE key, not half of it.


      NOT 2NF (key is (stud_id, course_id); but student_name depends on stud_id
      ALONE, and course_title on course_id ALONE - partial dependencies):
      +----------+-----------+--------------+---------------+--------+
      | stud_id  | course_id | student_name | course_title  | grade  |
      +----------+-----------+--------------+---------------+--------+
      | STU-0001 | ART101    | Sara Adib    | Drawing       | A      |
      | STU-0001 | MATH210   | Sara Adib    | Calculus      | B      |
      | STU-0002 | MATH210   | Omar Reyes   | Calculus      | A      |
      +----------+-----------+--------------+---------------+--------+
       "Sara Adib" and "Calculus" repeat - and could disagree between rows.
      2NF (each partial fact moves to the table of the key-part it depends on;
      only grade - which needs BOTH parts - stays on the bridge):
      STUDENT                 COURSE                  ENROLMENT
      +----------+---------+  +-----------+---------+ +----------+-----------+-----+
      | stud_id  | name    |  | course_id | title   | | stud_id  | course_id |grade|
      +----------+---------+  +-----------+---------+ +----------+-----------+-----+
      | STU-0001 | Sara    |  | ART101    | Drawing | | STU-0001 | ART101    | A   |
      | STU-0002 | Omar    |  | MATH210   | Calculus| | STU-0001 | MATH210   | B   |
      +----------+---------+  +-----------+---------+ | STU-0002 | MATH210   | A   |
                                                      +----------+-----------+-----+

    THIRD NORMAL FORM (3NF) - no transitive dependency.
      A non-key column must depend on the key, not on ANOTHER non-key column. If a
      Book table stores publisher_id AND publisher_address, the address really
      depends on the publisher, not on the book - so it belongs in a Publisher
      table. Facts about a publisher live with the publisher.

      NOT 3NF (book_id is the key, but publisher_name/address depend on pub_id,
      another non-key column - a transitive dependency book -> pub -> address):
      +--------+-----------+--------+----------------+---------------------+
      | book_id| title     | pub_id | publisher_name | publisher_address   |
      +--------+-----------+--------+----------------+---------------------+
      | B-1    | Dune      | PUB-2  | Gollancz       | 50 Victoria, London |
      | B-2    | Emma      | PUB-1  | Chatto         | 20 Vauxhall, London |
      | B-3    | The Hobbit| PUB-2  | Gollancz       | 50 Victoria, London |
      +--------+-----------+--------+----------------+---------------------+
       Gollancz's address repeats on B-1 and B-3 - change the address once and the
       rows disagree. 3NF (publisher facts move to a PUBLISHER table; the book just
       keeps the pointer):
      BOOK                                PUBLISHER
      +--------+-----------+--------+     +--------+-----------+---------------------+
      | book_id| title     | pub_id |     | pub_id | name      | address             |
      +--------+-----------+--------+     +--------+-----------+---------------------+
      | B-1    | Dune      | PUB-2  |     | PUB-1  | Chatto    | 20 Vauxhall, London |
      | B-2    | Emma      | PUB-1  |     | PUB-2  | Gollancz  | 50 Victoria, London |
      | B-3    | The Hobbit| PUB-2  |     +--------+-----------+---------------------+
      +--------+-----------+--------+     one row per publisher; address stored once


    (There is a stricter cousin, BCNF, and higher forms for rare cases. For almost
    all real work, "clean to 3NF" is the target and the rest is specialist.)

    THE MNEMONIC: every non-key fact must depend on "the key, the whole key, and
    nothing but the key." That single line is 1NF-2NF-3NF in a sentence.
    +------+--------------------------------+-------------------------------------+
    | form | the phrase                     | what it forbids                     |
    +------+--------------------------------+-------------------------------------+
    | 1NF  | (atomic cells)                 | many values in a cell; phone1,2,3   |
    | 2NF  | "...the WHOLE key..."          | a fact depending on PART of the key |
    | 3NF  | "...and NOTHING BUT the key."  | a fact depending on another non-key |
    +------+--------------------------------+-------------------------------------+

  WHEN TO STOP - and even step back
    Normalisation splits data into more, smaller tables. That is good for
    correctness but means more links to re-join when you ask questions, which can
    be slower. Sometimes, deliberately and knowingly, designers DENORMALISE - keep
    a little redundancy - for speed on read-heavy systems. Do it consciously, never
    by accident, and never before you have a measured reason. Correct first; fast
    later, only if needed.

    DENORMALISE vs a FROZEN FACT - do not confuse them. Both put a "copyable"
    value in a row, but for opposite reasons:
    +------------------+---------------------------+----------------------------+
    |                  | denormalised copy         | frozen fact                |
    +------------------+---------------------------+----------------------------+
    | why it is there  | speed; avoid a join       | it was TRUE at a moment and |
    |                  |                           | must not change later       |
    | may it drift     | no - must be kept in sync | it is SUPPOSED to differ    |
    | from the source? | with the source          | from today's value          |
    | example          | copy publisher_name onto | unit_price_at_sale on an    |
    |                  | book for fast lists       | order line (Part 9)         |
    +------------------+---------------------------+----------------------------+
    A frozen fact is not a normalisation sin - it is a DIFFERENT fact that happens
    to look like today's value. Part 9 gives it its due.

  >> CONTEMPLATE: go back to the loan table above and split it into clean tables.
     How many do you get, and what is the key and the foreign key of each? (You
     should reach Member, Book, and Loan - the bridge - with Loan carrying member_id
     and book_id. You just normalised a design by yourself.)


  THE SICK TABLE, CURED - the wide loan sheet from the top of this Part, split so
  each fact sits once. Follow the ids across the three tables:
    MEMBER                              BOOK
    +----------+-------------+--------+ +--------+------------+
    | memb_id  | name        | phone  | | book_id| title      |
    +----------+-------------+--------+ +--------+------------+
    | M-1001   | Sara Adib   |555-1020| | B-1    | Dune       |
    | M-1002   | Omar Reyes  |555-7788| | B-2    | Emma       |
    | M-1003   | Mina Lee    |555-3030| | B-3    | The Hobbit |
    +----------+-------------+--------+ +--------+------------+
    LOAN  (the bridge; member_id FK -> MEMBER, book_id FK -> BOOK)
    +---------+----------+---------+------------+
    | loan_id | memb_id  | book_id | due_date   |
    +---------+----------+---------+------------+
    | 1       | M-1001   | B-1     | 2026-10-14 |   Sara / Dune
    | 2       | M-1001   | B-2     | 2026-10-14 |   Sara / Emma
    | 3       | M-1002   | B-1     | 2026-10-20 |   Omar / Dune
    | 4       | M-1001   | B-3     | 2026-10-28 |   Sara / The Hobbit
    +---------+----------+---------+------------+
    Now watch the three anomalies die: Sara's phone is in ONE place (change it
    once); Mina (M-1003) exists with NO loans at all - no problem; delete loan 3
    and Omar still exists in MEMBER. One fact, one place, every symptom gone.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  PART 7 - THE METHOD: A REPEATABLE RECIPE
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Now that you own the pieces, here is the order to use them. Follow it top to
bottom the first few times; later it becomes instinct. Notice it moves from ideas
(steps 1-4) to tables (steps 8-9), exactly the level-1-to-level-2 descent of Part 1.

To make the steps concrete, we DO each one on a small running example as we go: a
CLINIC that books patients with doctors and records what gets prescribed. (The
full library example is Part 8; here the clinic shows the METHOD in motion.)

  STEP 1  STATE THE PURPOSE. Write, in sentences, what the system is for, who uses
          it, and - crucially - the QUESTIONS it must answer and ACTIONS it must
          support (Part 1). This list is your ruler for everything after.

     DOING IT (clinic):
     "A clinic books appointments between patients and doctors, and records the
      drugs prescribed at a visit."
     +----+----------------------------------------------+
     | Q1 | which appointments does Dr Khan have today?  |
     | Q2 | what was prescribed to patient P at visit V? |
     | Q3 | which patients has doctor D ever seen?       |
     | A1 | book an appointment; A2 record a prescription|
     +----+----------------------------------------------+

  STEP 2  FIND THE ENTITIES. Underline the NOUNS in your sentences. Group synonyms,
          drop the ones you keep no facts about. What's left are your candidate
          entity types (tables-to-be).

     DOING IT (clinic): nouns -> PATIENT, DOCTOR, APPOINTMENT (a visit; it will
     carry its own facts - a time), DRUG. "clinic" itself is just the whole system,
     not a table. Four candidate entities.

  STEP 3  FIND THE ATTRIBUTES. For each entity, list what you know about it. Mark
          which are composite (split them), which are multi-valued (they will
          become their own table), and which are derived (don't store them).

     DOING IT (clinic):
     +-------------+------------------------------------------------------------+
     | entity      | attributes (composite split; derived dropped)              |
     +-------------+------------------------------------------------------------+
     | PATIENT     | patient_id, given_name, family_name (split), birthdate     |
     | DOCTOR      | doctor_id, name, speciality                                |
     | APPOINTMENT | appt_id, patient_id, doctor_id, starts_at                  |
     | DRUG        | drug_id, name, strength                                    |
     +-------------+------------------------------------------------------------+
     (A patient's AGE is derived from birthdate - not stored.)


  STEP 4  FIND THE RELATIONSHIPS. Underline the VERBS joining nouns. For each, ask
          cardinality in BOTH directions and its participation (min/max, Part 2.4).
          Say each relationship aloud as two sentences and check both are true.

     DOING IT (clinic):
     +----------------------+-------------------------------------+-------+
     | relationship         | both directions                     | type  |
     +----------------------+-------------------------------------+-------+
     | PATIENT - APPOINTMENT| patient has many appts; appt 1 pat. | 1:N   |
     | DOCTOR  - APPOINTMENT| doctor has many appts; appt 1 doc.  | 1:N   |
     | APPOINTMENT - DRUG   | a visit prescribes many drugs; a    | M:N   |
     |                      | drug given at many visits           |       |
     +----------------------+-------------------------------------+-------+
     The M:N (appointment-drug) is a flag for Step 7.

  STEP 5  CHOOSE KEYS. Give each entity a primary key (usually a surrogate; keep
          natural identifiers as unique columns). Note where composite keys arise.

     DOING IT (clinic): surrogate ids patient_id, doctor_id, appt_id, drug_id. The
     bridge from Step 7 (prescription) will key on (appt_id, drug_id). Keep any
     natural code (e.g. an NHS number on patient) as a UNIQUE column, not the PK.

  STEP 6  DRAW IT. Put the entities in boxes, draw the relationship lines, mark the
          crow's-foot ends. Now you can SEE the whole model and critique it.

     DOING IT (clinic):
       +----------+                         +----------+
       | PATIENT  |--||==================o<--| APPOINT- |
       +----------+                         | MENT     |
       +----------+                         +----------+
       | DOCTOR   |--||==================o<--/   (APPOINTMENT sits at the many
       +----------+                            end of BOTH 1:N links)
       APPOINTMENT -->o================o<-- DRUG   (M:N, still to resolve)

  STEP 7  RESOLVE THE HARD PATTERNS. Turn every many-to-many into a bridge table;
          handle self-references, weak entities, and ternaries (Part 5). Move each
          relationship's own facts onto its bridge.

     DOING IT (clinic): the APPOINTMENT-DRUG many-to-many becomes a PRESCRIPTION
     bridge, holding the pairing's own facts (dose, instructions):
       APPOINTMENT ||--o< PRESCRIPTION >o--|| DRUG
     +----------+---------+--------------+---------------------+
     | appt_id  | drug_id | dose_mg      | instructions        |
     +----------+---------+--------------+---------------------+
     | AP-1     | D-AMOX  | 500          | 3x daily, 7 days    |
     | AP-1     | D-IBU   | 200          | as needed           |
     | AP-2     | D-AMOX  | 250          | 2x daily, 5 days    |
     +----------+---------+--------------+---------------------+
     PK = (appt_id, drug_id); or a surrogate prescription_id WITH UNIQUE(appt_id,
     drug_id) if the same drug is never listed twice on one visit.


  STEP 8  NORMALISE. Check each table to 3NF: "the key, the whole key, and nothing
          but the key" (Part 6). Split out any fact that is in the wrong place.

     DOING IT (clinic): is the patient's name on APPOINTMENT? No - it is on
     PATIENT (it depends on the patient, not the visit). Is the drug's name on
     PRESCRIPTION? No - it is on DRUG. dose_mg needs BOTH appt and drug, so it
     rightly stays on the bridge. Clean to 3NF.

  STEP 9  MAP TO TABLES. Apply the mapping rules below to write the final table
          list: names, columns, types, primary keys, foreign keys, and delete rules.

     DOING IT (clinic) - the final list:
       patient(patient_id PK, given_name, family_name, birthdate)
       doctor(doctor_id PK, name, speciality)
       appointment(appt_id PK, patient_id FK->patient, doctor_id FK->doctor,
                   starts_at)
       drug(drug_id PK, name, strength)
       prescription(appt_id FK->appointment, drug_id FK->drug, dose_mg,
                    instructions, PK=(appt_id, drug_id))
     ON DELETE: deleting an appointment CASCADEs to its prescriptions (they cannot
     outlive the visit); deleting a doctor is RESTRICTed while appointments exist.

  STEP 10 REVIEW AGAINST STEP 1. Walk every question and action from your purpose
          list through the design. Can each be answered? Iterate. First drafts are
          never final - good designs are rewritten, not born.

     DOING IT (clinic): Q1 "Dr Khan's appts today" = appointment rows with his
     doctor_id and today's date - yes. Q2 "prescribed to P at visit V" =
     prescription rows for that appt_id - yes. Q3 "patients doctor D has seen" =
     distinct patient_id over D's appointments - yes. All answerable; design holds.


  THE MAPPING RULES (ER model -> tables), collected in one place
    - Each ENTITY becomes a TABLE; each of its atomic attributes becomes a COLUMN.
    - Each entity's PRIMARY KEY becomes the table's primary key.
    - A ONE-TO-MANY relationship becomes a FOREIGN KEY column on the MANY side,
      pointing at the one side's primary key.
    - A ONE-TO-ONE becomes a foreign key on either side (put it on the optional
      side), often with a UNIQUE rule so it stays one-to-one.
    - A MANY-TO-MANY becomes a NEW bridge table whose primary key is the pair of
      foreign keys (plus any facts about the pairing).
    - A MULTI-VALUED attribute becomes its own table, one row per value, linked by
      a foreign key back to the owner.
    - A SELF-REFERENCE becomes a foreign key column pointing back into the same
      table (parent_id), NULL at the root.
    - For each foreign key, decide the ON-DELETE rule: cascade (children die with
      the parent), set-null (link clears, child survives), or restrict (forbid
      deleting while children exist). This encodes what the world allows.

    The same rules as a lookup table - "when I see THIS in the model, I write
    THAT in the tables":
    +-------------------------------+------------------------------------------+
    | in the ER model               | in the tables                            |
    +-------------------------------+------------------------------------------+
    | entity                        | a table                                  |
    | atomic attribute              | a column                                 |
    | primary key                   | the table's PRIMARY KEY                   |
    | 1:N relationship              | FK on the many side -> one side's PK     |
    | 1:1 relationship              | FK on one side + UNIQUE                   |
    | M:N relationship              | a new bridge table (two FKs = its PK)    |
    | multi-valued attribute        | its own table, one row per value         |
    | self-reference (tree)         | parent_id FK into the same table, NULL   |
    |                               | at the root                              |
    | each FK                       | pick ON DELETE: cascade/set-null/restrict|
    +-------------------------------+------------------------------------------+

  KEY IDEA: the recipe never changes - purpose, entities, attributes, relationships,
  keys, draw, resolve patterns, normalise, map, review. Memorise the ten steps and
  you can design anything, because you will always know what to do next.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  PART 8 - A COMPLETE WORKED EXAMPLE: A SMALL COMMUNITY LIBRARY
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Let us run the whole recipe once, start to finish, on something small. Read it
with your own sheet and try each step before reading my version. This is the
example to come back to: it is drawn ALL the way down to sample rows and answered
questions, so you can see a finished design actually working.

  STEP 1 - PURPOSE. A small library lends books to members. It must answer:
    "which books does this member have out now?", "is any copy of this title
    available?", "who is overdue?", "how many times has this title been borrowed?"
    Actions: register a member, lend a copy, take a return.

  STEP 2 - ENTITIES (nouns underlined in the purpose): MEMBER, BOOK (the title/work),
    COPY (a physical volume - a title can have several), and - foreseeing a verb
    with its own facts - LOAN. Four entities.

  STEP 3 - ATTRIBUTES.
    MEMBER: id, full name (composite - store given/family), email (unique),
            joined_on. (Age? derived from nothing stored - skip; we don't need it.)
    BOOK:   id, title, author, isbn (natural, unique). (One author, to stay small;
            note the simplification - a real library would make Author an entity.)
    COPY:   id, the book it is a copy of (link), shelf code, acquired_on.
    LOAN:   id, which copy, which member, borrowed_on, due_on, returned_on (NULL
            until returned - its emptiness MEANS "still out").

  STEP 4 - RELATIONSHIPS and their cardinality (say each aloud, both ways):
    BOOK ||--o< COPY      one title has many copies; each copy is of one title.
    MEMBER ||--o< LOAN     a member may have many loans (and zero); each loan, one
                           member.
    COPY ||--o< LOAN       a copy has many loans over time; each loan, one copy.
    Notice LOAN sits at the "many" end of two 1:N links - it is the bridge between
    MEMBER and COPY, and it carries its own facts (the dates). It earned its place.


  STEP 5 - KEYS. Surrogate id on each table (member_id, book_id, copy_id, loan_id).
    Keep email UNIQUE on member and isbn UNIQUE on book as natural identifiers.

  STEP 6 - DRAW IT (in text):
       Member ||---o< Loan >o---|| Copy >o---|| Book
    Read the chain: a member has zero-or-many loans; each loan has exactly one
    copy; a copy has zero-or-many loans and belongs to exactly one book; a book has
    zero-or-many copies. Every sentence is true of a real library - the design holds.

    The same model, drawn as boxes so you can see all four at once:
    +----------+                        +------------+                 +--------+
    |  MEMBER  |--||================o<--|   LOAN     |--o<=========||--| COPY   |
    +----------+                        +------------+                 +--------+
      one member                        each loan: one                 each copy:
      zero-or-many loans                member, one copy               many loans
                                                                       over time,
                                        +--------+                     one book
                                        |  BOOK  |--||==============o<--+
                                        +--------+
                                          one book has zero-or-many copies
       Read aloud: MEMBER ||--o< LOAN (one member, many loans); LOAN >o--|| COPY
       (each loan one copy, a copy many loans); COPY >o--|| BOOK (each copy one
       book, a book many copies). All true -> keep.

  STEP 7 - HARD PATTERNS. The Member-Copy many-to-many (a member borrows many
    copies over time; a copy is borrowed by many members) is already resolved: LOAN
    is its bridge, and it holds the pairing's own facts (the dates). Nothing else to
    resolve - no self-reference, no ternary here.

  STEP 8 - NORMALISE. Check "the key, the whole key, nothing but the key":
    Loan holds copy_id, member_id, and dates - all facts about THAT loan: good.
    The member's name is NOT on Loan (it depends on the member, not the loan): good.
    The book's title is NOT on Copy (it depends on the book): good. We are at 3NF.


  STEP 9 - THE FINAL TABLES:
    member(member_id PK, given_name, family_name, email UNIQUE, joined_on)
    book(book_id PK, title, author, isbn UNIQUE)
    copy(copy_id PK, book_id FK->book, shelf_code, acquired_on)
    loan(loan_id PK, copy_id FK->copy, member_id FK->member,
         borrowed_on, due_on, returned_on NULL)

  STEP 9b - THE FINAL TABLES, WITH SAMPLE DATA. Here is the whole little library
    as rows. The ids are consistent across tables, so you can trace any link by
    hand. (Pretend "today" is 2026-10-05 for the overdue question later.)

    member
    +----------+------------+-------------+--------------------+------------+
    | member_id| given_name | family_name | email (UNIQUE)     | joined_on  |
    +----------+------------+-------------+--------------------+------------+
    | M-1001   | Sara       | Adib        | sara@example.org   | 2025-01-10 |
    | M-1002   | Omar       | Reyes       | omar@example.org   | 2025-03-22 |

    +----------+------------+-------------+--------------------+------------+
    (M-1003 Mina has joined but never borrowed - and that is perfectly storable.)

    book

    copy  (book_id FK -> book)

    loan  (copy_id FK -> copy, member_id FK -> member; today = 2026-10-05)
    +--------+--------+----------+------------+------------+-------------+

  STEP 10 - REVIEW against Step 1's questions:
    "Books this member has out now?" loans where member_id = ? and returned_on is
      NULL, joined to copy and book. Answerable.
    "Is a copy of this title available?" copies of the book with no open loan.
      Answerable. "Who is overdue?" open loans with due_on in the past. Answerable.
    "How many times borrowed?" count loans joined to this book. Answerable. It
    passes - and every table traces back to a real question. That is a finished
    small design, built by the recipe. student-panel is this same method, larger.


  THE DESIGN AT WORK - the four questions, ANSWERED by hand against the rows above.
  This is the payoff: watch the links do real work.

    Q "Which books does Sara (M-1001) have out now?"
      -> loan rows with member_id = M-1001 AND returned_on IS NULL:  L-1, L-2.
         L-1.copy_id = C-2 -> copy C-2.book_id = B-1 -> book "Dune".
         L-2.copy_id = C-3 -> copy C-3.book_id = B-2 -> book "Emma".
      ANSWER: Sara has Dune and Emma out. (Her old Hobbit loan L-5 is returned, so
      it does not count - returned_on is filled in.)

    Q "Is any copy of The Hobbit (B-3) available right now?"
      -> copies of B-3: C-4 and C-5.
         C-4 has an OPEN loan (L-3, returned_on NULL) -> C-4 is out.
         C-5 has no open loan (its only loan L-5 is returned) -> C-5 is free.
      ANSWER: yes - copy C-5 is available.

    Q "Who is overdue?" (today = 2026-10-05)
      -> open loans (returned_on NULL) whose due_on is before today:
         L-1 due 2026-10-14 -> future, fine.
         L-2 due 2026-10-04 -> PAST -> overdue. member M-1001 = Sara.
         L-3 due 2026-10-13 -> future, fine.
      ANSWER: Sara is overdue (Emma, due 2026-10-04).

    Q "How many times has Dune (B-1) been borrowed, ever?"
      -> copies of B-1: C-1, C-2. Count ALL loans (open or returned) on those:
         C-2 -> L-1 ;  C-1 -> L-4.
      ANSWER: 2 times. (Note we count history too - the returned L-4 still counts,
      because the question is "ever," not "now." Different question, different
      filter, SAME tables. That flexibility is the reward for a clean design.)


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  PART 9 - DESIGN JUDGEMENT: THE DECISIONS WITH NO SINGLE RIGHT ANSWER
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The recipe gives you a correct design. Judgement makes it a GOOD one. These are the
recurring trade-offs - the "what could we do differently, and should this exist at
all?" layer. None has one universal answer; each has a question to ask yourself.

  DERIVE vs STORE. If a fact can be computed from others (a total, an age, a count),
    prefer to compute it when asked, so it can never fall out of step. Store it only
    when computing it is genuinely too slow AND you accept the duty to keep it
    fresh. Ask: "can this contradict the facts it is derived from?" If yes, don't
    store it.

  THE FROZEN-FACT EXCEPTION - the one time you DO store a copy. A value you capture
    ON PURPOSE at a moment in time is not a derived value; it is a historical fact
    that must never change, even when the "live" value does. The classic case is
    the price on a sale: the product's price today may be 1.50, but the line of a
    sale from last year must forever say what was CHARGED then.

    Drawn - the PRODUCT price can change; the ORDER_LINE price is frozen:
    PRODUCT (price today)              ORDER_LINE (price AS CHARGED, frozen)
    +--------+----------+--------+     +---------+--------+-----+------------------+
    | prod_id| name     | price  |     | order_id| prod_id| qty | unit_price_at_sale|
    +--------+----------+--------+     +---------+--------+-----+------------------+
    | PR-1   | Mug      | 9.00   |     | O-500   | PR-1   | 2   | 7.50  (old price)|
    | PR-2   | Notebook | 4.00   |     | O-500   | PR-2   | 1   | 4.00             |
    +--------+----------+--------+     | O-700   | PR-1   | 1   | 9.00  (new price)|
       Mug is 9.00 TODAY...            +---------+--------+-----+------------------+
       ...but order O-500 charged 7.50 for it last season, and must keep saying so.
    unit_price_at_sale is NOT a 3NF violation and NOT lazy denormalisation: it is a
    SEPARATE fact ("the price charged on this line") that merely resembles today's
    price. The ORDER TOTAL, by contrast, IS derived - sum of qty*unit_price_at_sale
    across the lines - so you compute it, you do NOT store it.
    (student-panel parallel: a recorded SCORE is a frozen fact of its period - what
    was recorded then stays what was recorded then, even if parameters later change.)


  WHAT NULL MEANS. An empty (NULL) value is not zero and not "" - it is "unknown or
    not-applicable." Before you allow a column to be empty, decide what its
    emptiness will MEAN (loan.returned_on being NULL means "still out"). A NULL you
    can't explain in words is a design you haven't finished thinking about.

    Decide this per column, and write the meaning down:
    +----------------------+-------------+--------------------------------------+
    | column               | NULL allowed| if NULL, it MEANS...                  |
    +----------------------+-------------+--------------------------------------+
    | loan.returned_on     | yes         | "not yet returned - still out"       |
    | member.email         | no (NOT NULL)| (blank email would be nonsense)     |
    | member.middle_name   | yes         | "has no middle name / not recorded"  |
    | employee.manager_id  | yes         | "this is the root - reports to none" |
    | order_line.order_id  | no (NOT NULL)| (a line with no order cannot exist) |
    +----------------------+-------------+--------------------------------------+
    Where blank would be nonsense, forbid it with NOT NULL. Where blank carries a
    real meaning, allow it - and record what the meaning is, as above.

  SOFT vs HARD DELETE. Deleting a row really removes it (hard). Sometimes you instead
    mark it "deleted_at = now" and hide it (soft delete), so history and references
    survive and mistakes are recoverable. Soft delete keeps the past but complicates
    every query ("...and not deleted"). Ask: "will anyone ever need to know this
    existed?" student-panel soft-deletes students and archives parameters for exactly
    this reason.

    Drawn - a soft-delete column; the row stays, but queries hide it:
    +----------+-------------+------------+---------------------------+
    | student_id| name       | is_active  | archived_at               |
    +----------+-------------+------------+---------------------------+
    | STU-0001 | Sara Adib   | true       | (null)                    |  shown
    | STU-0002 | Omar Reyes  | true       | (null)                    |  shown
    | STU-0003 | Mina Lee    | false      | 2026-09-15                |  HIDDEN
    +----------+-------------+------------+---------------------------+
    Mina's row is not gone - her old scores still point at a real student, and she
    can be restored. The cost: every normal query must add "WHERE is_active" or it
    will show her. Hard delete is simpler but forgets forever. Choose on purpose.


  A FLEXIBLE (JSON) COLUMN vs PROPER COLUMNS. Most engines let one column hold a
    free-form bag of data (JSON). It is tempting for "extra, varies-per-row" fields.
    But data in there can't be checked, keyed, or easily searched by the database.
    Rule of thumb: facts you will filter, sort, or enforce deserve real columns;
    only genuinely open-ended extras belong in a flexible column. Reach for it
    rarely and on purpose.

    +------------------------------+-------------------+------------------------+
    | the fact                     | real column?      | why                    |
    +------------------------------+-------------------+------------------------+
    | student birthdate            | REAL column       | you sort/filter by it  |
    | order status                 | REAL column       | you filter by it; rules|
    | which colour a user picked   | REAL column       | small, fixed, queryable|
    | arbitrary per-import notes    | JSON column       | open-ended, never      |
    |                              |                   | queried, varies wildly |
    +------------------------------+-------------------+------------------------+

  NAMING. Consistent, boring names are a feature: singular-or-plural table names
    (pick one), id for the primary key, thing_id for a foreign key, lower_snake_case
    throughout. Future-you reads the schema far more often than you write it.

    AVOID RESERVED WORDS. Database engines reserve words like order, group, user,
    select, table for their own grammar. Name a table one of these and every query
    needs awkward quoting - or breaks. Pick a safe synonym or a plural. student-panel
    runs on PostgreSQL, where exactly these words bite:
    +------------------+----------------------+-----------------------------------+
    | tempting name    | safe name            | note                              |
    +------------------+----------------------+-----------------------------------+
    | order            | orders               | "order" is reserved (ORDER BY)    |
    | group            | groups               | "group" is reserved (GROUP BY)    |
    | user             | app_user  / users    | "user" is reserved in Postgres    |
    | class            | classes / cohort     | reserved in some engines          |
    | select, table    | (never)              | always reserved - rename the idea |
    +------------------+----------------------+-----------------------------------+
    The habit: plural table names (orders, groups, students) sidestep most reserved
    words for free, and read naturally ("rows in students").

  KEY IDEA: correctness is teachable in ten steps; judgement grows by making these
    calls, stating your reason, and later seeing how it played out. That is exactly
    what our compare-against-the-reference loop is for.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  GLOSSARY - quick reference
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  ENTITY (TYPE)     a kind of thing you keep facts about; becomes a TABLE.
  ENTITY INSTANCE   one specific such thing; becomes a ROW.
  ATTRIBUTE         a fact about an entity; becomes a COLUMN.
    atomic          holds one indivisible value.
    composite       really several values (address -> street, city, postcode).
    multi-valued    has many values at once -> needs its own rows/table.
    derived         computable from other facts -> prefer not to store.
  RELATIONSHIP      an association between entities; the VERB. Degree = how many
                    entity types it joins (unary/binary/ternary).
  CARDINALITY       how many relate to how many: 1:1, 1:N, M:N (the maximum side).
  PARTICIPATION     the minimum side: is zero allowed (optional) or required
                    (mandatory)?
  CANDIDATE KEY     any attribute set that uniquely identifies a row.
  PRIMARY KEY       the one candidate chosen as the official identifier; unique,
                    never empty, ideally never changing.
  NATURAL KEY       a real-world identifier (ISBN, email).
  SURROGATE KEY     a meaningless invented identifier (auto-number, UUID).
  COMPOSITE KEY     a key made of more than one column.
  FOREIGN KEY       a column holding another table's primary-key value; a pointer.
  REFERENTIAL       the database's guarantee that a foreign key points at a real
    INTEGRITY       row (or is empty, if optional).
  BRIDGE / JUNCTION /  a table in the middle that resolves a many-to-many into two
    ASSOCIATIVE table    one-to-manys; holds the pairing's own facts.
  WEAK ENTITY       a thing that cannot exist without its parent (order-line).
  SELF-REFERENCE    a foreign key pointing back into the same table (parent_id);
                    builds trees/hierarchies of any depth.
  NORMALISATION     organising tables so each fact lives in exactly one place.
    1NF             atomic cells, no repeating columns.
    2NF             no non-key fact depends on only PART of a composite key.
    3NF             no non-key fact depends on another non-key fact.
  ANOMALY           an update/insert/delete problem caused by redundancy.
  DENORMALISE       deliberately keep some redundancy for read speed (with care).
  SOFT DELETE       mark a row deleted (deleted_at) instead of removing it.
  NULL              "unknown or not-applicable" - decide what each NULL MEANS.
  CONCEPTUAL /      the three design heights: ideas -> tables -> real storage.
    LOGICAL / PHYSICAL
  FROZEN FACT       a value copied ON PURPOSE at a moment and never changed again
                    (unit_price_at_sale; a recorded score). NOT the same as a
                    derived value and NOT denormalisation - it is history.
  UNIQUE            a constraint: no two rows may share this value (student_code,
    (CONSTRAINT)    email). Guards natural keys even when the PK is a surrogate.
  NOT NULL          a constraint: this column must always hold a value.
  ON DELETE         what happens to child rows when their parent is deleted:
    RESTRICT        refuse the delete while children exist (the safe default).
    CASCADE         delete the children too (use only when they can't outlive it).
    SET NULL        keep the children but empty their foreign key (must be optional)
  ORPHAN            a child row whose foreign key points at a parent that no longer
                    exists; referential integrity exists to prevent these.
  RESERVED WORD     a word the database grammar owns (order, group, user, select);
                    don't name tables/columns these - use orders, groups, app_user.
  HARD DELETE       actually remove the row (opposite of SOFT DELETE); frees space
                    but loses history and can orphan children - choose per table.

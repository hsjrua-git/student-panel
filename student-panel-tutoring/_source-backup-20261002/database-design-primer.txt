This primer teaches the CRAFT of designing a database: how you get from "here is
a situation in the real world" to "here are the tables, and here is why they
look like this." It uses small, everyday examples - a library, a school, a shop
- on purpose. student-panel is an advanced, finished design; you will design far
better once you have built the method here first, on things small enough to hold
in your head.

Nothing here asks you to touch a computer. Design happens on paper, in your head,
in conversation. Read slowly. Keep a pencil and a blank sheet beside you.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  PART 0 - HOW TO USE THIS PRIMER
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHAT THIS IS
  A complete beginner's course in database DESIGN and, at its heart, in ER
  ("entity-relationship") modelling - the standard way people think about and
  draw a database before they build it. We go biggest-picture first, then
  descend from major ideas to minor details, and we do that descent again inside
  each part. Before every piece we pause on four questions: what is it, how does
  it work, what could (and could not) be done with it, and should it exist at
  all.

WHAT THIS IS NOT
  It is not about student-panel yet, and it is not a SQL syntax manual. You will
  meet a little SQL near the end, only to show how a design becomes real tables.
  The thinking comes first; the typing comes much later.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  PART 1 - WHAT "DESIGNING A DATABASE" ACTUALLY MEANS  (the biggest picture)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Start as high up as we can. A database is a place that remembers facts for you,
in an organised way, so a program can store them and ask questions of them
later. Designing one means deciding WHAT facts you will keep and HOW they are
arranged. That is it. Everything technical below serves those two decisions.

Here is the mental move at the centre of all of it: a database is a small,
deliberate MODEL of a slice of the real world. The real world is infinitely
detailed; your model keeps only the parts your program cares about and throws
the rest away. A library's database "knows" a book has a title and an author,
but not that its cover is scuffed or that it smells of coffee. Design is the art
of choosing what to keep.

WHY NOT JUST ONE BIG SPREADSHEET?
  Because facts repeat, and repetition rots. Put a member's phone number next to
  every book they borrow, and the day they change numbers you must find and fix
  it in twenty places - miss one and your data now disagrees with itself. A good
  design stores each fact ONCE, in the one place it belongs, and links to it from
  everywhere else. Most of this primer is really about that single sentence.

THE THREE LEVELS OF A DESIGN
  Designers deliberately think at three heights, from ideas down to storage:
    1. CONCEPTUAL - the ideas and their connections, in plain language, with no
       thought of any particular database product. "A member borrows many
       copies; a copy belongs to one book." This is where ER modelling lives.
    2. LOGICAL - those ideas turned into tables, columns, keys and links, still
       without worrying which database engine runs it.
    3. PHYSICAL - the real thing on a real engine: exact data types, indexes,
       storage. This is last, and it is the least interesting part of design.
  We spend almost all our time at levels 1 and 2. Beginners rush to level 3
  (typing commands); good designers linger at level 1 (getting the ideas right).

WHERE "ER" COMES FROM
  In 1976 Peter Chen proposed describing data as ENTITIES (things) joined by
  RELATIONSHIPS (how the things relate). It stuck because it matches how people
  already talk: nouns and verbs. An ER diagram is just a picture of nouns and
  verbs. You already think this way; we are only making it precise.

  KEY IDEA: a database is a chosen model of reality; design is deciding what to
  keep and how to arrange it so every fact lives in exactly one place.

WHAT MAKES A DESIGN "GOOD"?
  Four things, roughly in order of importance:
    - FAITHFUL: it can represent every real situation you care about, and it
      forbids situations that can't really happen (a loan with no book).
    - NON-REDUNDANT: each fact is stored once, so it can never contradict itself.
    - ANSWERABLE: the questions you will actually ask are easy and fast to ask.
    - SIMPLE: no table, column, or link exists without a reason you can state.
  Notice these can pull against each other - chasing zero redundancy can make
  questions harder to ask. Design is the judgement that balances them. There is
  rarely one "right" schema, only better and worse trade-offs for a purpose.

BEGIN FROM THE QUESTIONS, NOT THE TABLES
  The single most useful habit: before drawing anything, write down the questions
  the system must answer and the actions it must support. "Which books is this
  member holding right now?" "How many copies of this title do we own?" "Who had
  this book before it was returned damaged?" A design is good exactly to the
  degree it answers ITS questions well. Tables you can't tie back to a real
  question are usually tables that shouldn't exist.

  >> CONTEMPLATE: imagine a tiny system for a coffee shop's loyalty cards. Before
     reading on, write five questions it must answer and two actions it must
     support. Keep the list - we test designs against lists like this.

  >> PITFALL: starting by asking "what columns do I need?" That is level-3
     thinking wearing a disguise. Start at level 1: what THINGS exist, and how do
     they relate? Columns come later and almost fall out on their own.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  PART 2 - THE THREE PRIMITIVES: ENTITIES, ATTRIBUTES, RELATIONSHIPS
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Almost every ER model is built from just three kinds of thing. Learn these three
deeply and the rest of design is refinement. We take them major-to-minor.

  2.1  ENTITIES - the "things"
  ----------------------------
  An entity is a thing your system needs to remember: a Member, a Book, an Order,
  a Student, a Room. If you can point at it and say "we keep facts about that,"
  it is a candidate entity. In speech, entities are usually the NOUNS.

  Two words you must not confuse:
    - ENTITY TYPE (or entity set): the KIND of thing - "Book". This becomes a
      TABLE. When people say "entity" they usually mean this.
    - ENTITY INSTANCE: one particular one - "Book #4417, 'Dune'". This becomes a
      ROW in that table.
  A table is the type; each row is an instance. Hold that distinction; a lot of
  confusion later is really this confusion.

  WHAT IS *NOT* AN ENTITY (yet)
    - A mere property of something else ("a book's title") - that is an ATTRIBUTE,
      not an entity of its own... usually. See the contemplation below; the line
      moves.
    - An action ("borrowing") - that is usually a RELATIONSHIP. But actions worth
      remembering in detail often become entities too (a "Loan"). Judgement again.

  >> CONTEMPLATE: is COLOUR an entity or an attribute? For a car-rental app,
     colour is probably just an attribute of Car. For a paint manufacturer, Colour
     is a full entity with a code, a formula, a supplier. Same word, different
     model - because the PURPOSE differs. What decides it? Ask: do we keep facts
     ABOUT the colour itself? If yes, it wants to be an entity.

  KEY IDEA: entity type = table = a kind of thing; entity instance = row = one
  specific thing. Entities are the nouns you keep facts about.

  2.2  ATTRIBUTES - what we know about a thing
  --------------------------------------------
  An attribute is a fact stored about an entity: a Member's name, a Book's title,
  a Student's birthdate. In tables, attributes become COLUMNS. Choosing good
  attributes is choosing what your model notices about the world.

  Attributes come in kinds worth naming, because each changes how you model it:

    - ATOMIC (simple) vs COMPOSITE. Atomic means "one indivisible value" - a
      price, a title. Composite means it is really several values wearing one
      name - "address" is street + city + postcode. Composites usually get split
      into separate columns, because you will want to search or sort by the parts.

    - SINGLE-VALUED vs MULTI-VALUED. A person has one birthdate (single) but may
      have many phone numbers (multi). This one matters enormously: a table cell
      holds ONE value, so a multi-valued attribute CANNOT simply be a column. It
      almost always signals a new entity or a link table. Remember this; it is the
      seed of the most common design pattern of all (Part 5).

    - STORED vs DERIVED. Some facts are stored directly (birthdate); others can be
      COMPUTED from stored ones (age = today - birthdate). Derived facts are best
      NOT stored - you recompute them when asked, so they can never drift out of
      date. "Derive, don't store" is a rule you will meet again and again.

    - KEY attributes - the ones that identify the thing. Important enough to get
      their own part (Part 3).

  >> CONTEMPLATE: "full name" - atomic or composite? If you will ever sort by
     surname or greet someone by first name, it is composite: store the parts.
     If you truly only ever print it whole, atomic is fine. The USE decides.

  >> PITFALL: a column holding "555-1234, 555-9988" (two phone numbers jammed in
     one cell). That is a multi-valued attribute in disguise, and it breaks the
     first rule of tidy tables (Part 6). One value per cell, always.

  2.3  RELATIONSHIPS - how the things connect
  -------------------------------------------
  A relationship is an association between entities: a Member BORROWS a Copy; a
  Book HAS a Publisher; a Student ENROLS IN a Course. In speech, relationships are
  the VERBS joining your nouns.

  A relationship's DEGREE is how many entity types it joins:
    - UNARY (recursive): an entity related to itself. An Employee MANAGES another
      Employee; a Category is the PARENT OF another Category. This is how trees and
      hierarchies are built, and it looks strange the first time - one box with a
      line looping back to itself. (Part 5 returns to it; student-panel leans on
      it heavily.)
    - BINARY: two entity types - by far the most common. Member-Loan, Book-Author.
    - TERNARY (and higher): three at once. "A Doctor prescribes a Drug to a
      Patient" links three things in one fact. These are rarer and usually get
      re-expressed as an entity in the middle (Part 5).

  Relationships can carry attributes of their OWN. "Member borrows Copy" - but the
  borrow DATE and the due DATE belong to neither the member nor the copy; they
  belong to the borrowing itself. A relationship with its own facts is a strong
  hint that the relationship wants to become an entity (an associative entity -
  here, a "Loan"). Spotting this early saves redesigns later.

  >> CONTEMPLATE: "A Student has a Grade in a Course." Whose fact is the grade?
     Not the student alone (they have many), not the course alone (it gives many).
     It belongs to the student-and-course PAIR. Where, then, must it be stored?

  KEY IDEA: entities are nouns, attributes are their adjectives, relationships are
  the verbs - and verbs can have facts of their own.

  2.4  CARDINALITY - the most important question about any relationship
  ---------------------------------------------------------------------
  For every relationship you must answer: how many of each side relate to how
  many of the other? This is CARDINALITY, and it decides the whole table layout.

  Ask it in BOTH directions, one side at a time, in plain English:
    "One member borrows how many copies?"  -> many.
    "One copy is borrowed by how many members (at once)?" -> one.
  That pair of answers ("many" / "one") makes this a ONE-TO-MANY relationship.

  The three shapes you will meet, with a test question for each:
    - ONE-TO-ONE (1:1). One country has one capital city; one capital belongs to
      one country. Rare, and often a hint the two entities could be one - or are
      split on purpose (optional or sensitive data kept apart).
    - ONE-TO-MANY (1:N). One publisher prints many books; each book has one
      publisher. The workhorse of database design; most links are these.
    - MANY-TO-MANY (M:N). One student takes many courses; one course has many
      students. These CANNOT be stored directly and always need a helper table -
      the single most important pattern in Part 5.

  PARTICIPATION (the "minimum" side of cardinality)
    Cardinality has a maximum (one/many) AND a minimum (must there be at least
    one, or is zero allowed?). "Must every loan have a book?" - yes, MANDATORY.
    "Must every member have a loan?" - no, a new member has zero, OPTIONAL. The
    minimum is what NULL and "optional" are really about, and it is where a design
    says which situations are allowed to exist at all.

  So each end of a relationship carries a (minimum, maximum) pair. Four little
  symbols in the next part encode exactly these pairs. Get cardinality right and
  the diagram almost draws itself; get it wrong and no amount of later cleverness
  saves the design.

  >> CONTEMPLATE: for your coffee-shop loyalty system - one card belongs to how
     many customers? one customer holds how many cards? Is a customer allowed to
     exist with zero cards? Your answers ARE the design of that link.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  PART 3 - KEYS: HOW A DATABASE KNOWS "WHICH ONE"
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Two rows that look alike are a disaster waiting to happen. Keys are how a database
tells things apart and how one table points reliably at a row in another. Identity
is the quiet foundation everything else stands on.

  CANDIDATE KEYS - any attribute (or set of attributes) whose value is unique for
  every row and never empty could serve to identify the row. A member might have
  several: their membership number, and perhaps their email. Each is a CANDIDATE.

  PRIMARY KEY - you pick ONE candidate to be the official identifier. Every table
  gets exactly one primary key; it is unique, never NULL, and ideally never
  changes. Everything that points at this row will point using this value.

  WHAT MAKES A GOOD PRIMARY KEY? It should be:
    - UNIQUE (by definition), and guaranteed so, not just "probably".
    - STABLE - it should never need to change. (This rules out a lot of tempting
      choices, see below.)
    - MINIMAL and MEANINGLESS is often best - the less real-world baggage it
      carries, the less reason it will ever have to change.

  >> CONTEMPLATE: should a person's name be the primary key? Names repeat (two
     John Smiths), names change (marriage), names have no guaranteed format. Every
     property of a good key is violated. So - almost never. What about email?
     Unique-ish, but people change emails, and then everything pointing at them
     breaks. This tension leads straight to the next idea.

  NATURAL vs SURROGATE KEYS - a real design fork
    A NATURAL key is a real-world value that already identifies the thing: an
    ISBN for a book, a country code, a membership number printed on the card.
    A SURROGATE key is a meaningless value the system invents purely to identify
    the row: an auto-incrementing number (1, 2, 3...) or a random UUID like
    3f2a...-.... It means nothing outside the database.

    Trade-offs:
      - Natural keys need no extra column and read meaningfully, BUT they change
        (and then every reference must change too), and "guaranteed unique
        forever" is rarer than it looks.
      - Surrogate keys never change and are always clean, BUT they carry no
        meaning, so you still keep the natural value as an ordinary (indexed,
        unique) column for humans to search by.
    Most modern designs use a surrogate primary key (auto-number or UUID) AND keep
    the natural identifier as a separate UNIQUE column - best of both. student-panel
    does exactly this: UUID primary keys, plus a human "student_code" kept unique.

  COMPOSITE KEYS - sometimes identity takes more than one column. In a table that
  records "student X is a member of group Y", the pair (student, group) together
  is the identity - neither alone is unique. A key made of several columns is a
  COMPOSITE key, and it is completely normal, especially in the link tables of
  Part 5.

  >> CONTEMPLATE: UUID vs a simple counting number for a primary key. The counter
     is smaller and human-readable but reveals how many rows exist and collides if
     you ever merge two databases. A UUID is bulky and ugly but unique across every
     machine on earth and leaks nothing. Which fits a small library? Which fits an
     app that might sync between devices? (This is a real student-panel choice.)

  FOREIGN KEYS - how a relationship becomes real
    A relationship in your head becomes, in tables, a FOREIGN KEY: a column in one
    table that holds the primary-key value of a row in another table. The Loan
    table has a book_id column whose value is the id of some row in Book. That
    column is a foreign key; it POINTS. Following it is how the database re-joins
    what your design split apart.

    REFERENTIAL INTEGRITY is the promise the database enforces: a foreign key must
    point at a row that actually exists (or be empty, if the link is optional). It
    will refuse to store a loan for book #9999 if no such book exists, and it will
    stop you deleting a book that still has loans - unless you tell it what should
    happen instead. Those "what happens on delete" rules (cascade the delete,
    set the link empty, or forbid the delete) are themselves design decisions
    about what the world allows.

  WHERE DOES THE FOREIGN KEY GO? A rule you can lean on:
    - In a ONE-TO-MANY, the key goes on the MANY side. Each book has one publisher,
      so book carries publisher_id - not the other way round. (Put it on the "one"
      side and you would need many values in one cell, which we forbade.)
    - In a ONE-TO-ONE, it can go on either side; put it where participation is
      optional, or where it reads most naturally.
    - In a MANY-TO-MANY, neither side can hold it - which forces the helper table
      of Part 5, and the foreign keys live THERE.

  KEY IDEA: a primary key is a row's name; a foreign key is another row saying
  that name to point at it. Relationships are just keys pointing at keys.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  PART 4 - READING AND DRAWING ER DIAGRAMS
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A picture lets you see a whole design at once and spot what is wrong. There are a
couple of notations; we use the CROW'S FOOT style, because it is the most common
today and it is what your student-panel diagram uses.

  THE PIECES
    - Each ENTITY is a box. Inside, one line per attribute (many tools also tag
      the key attributes). The box is the table.
    - A RELATIONSHIP is a LINE between two boxes.
    - The ENDS of the line carry the cardinality - read each end as the (minimum,
      maximum) pair from Part 2.4. Three little symbols say it all:
         a single bar   |    means "one"          (the maximum is one)
         a crow's foot  <    means "many"          (the maximum is many)
         a circle       o    means "zero/optional" (the minimum is zero)
      Combine the inner (min) and outer (max) symbol at each end:
         |o  = "zero or one"      (optional, at most one)
         ||  = "exactly one"      (mandatory, exactly one)
         o<  = "zero or many"     (optional, many)
         |<  = "one or many"      (mandatory, at least one)

  READING A LINE
    You read a crow's-foot line from each side toward the other. For
       Publisher ||--o< Book
    read left-to-right: "one publisher has zero-or-many books," and right-to-left:
    "each book has exactly one publisher." Both sentences must be TRUE of the real
    world - saying them aloud is the best test of your own diagram.

  THE COMMON SHAPES, DRAWN
    1:1   Country ||--|| Capital        one and only one each way
    1:N   Publisher ||--o< Book          one publisher, many books
    M:N   Student >o--o< Course          many each way - needs a helper (Part 5)
    optional 1:N  Member ||--o< Loan, but Loan ||--|| Copy: a member may have no
          loans (o), yet every loan must have exactly one copy (||). The little
          circle vs bar near the box is where "is this allowed to be empty?" lives.

  A TINY WORKED DIAGRAM (in text)
    Author ||--o< Book           one author writes many books; each book, one author
    Book   ||--o< Copy           a title has many physical copies; each copy, one title
    Read those four sentences aloud. If any is false for a real library (maybe a
    book has several authors?), the diagram is wrong and must change - better to
    find it now, on paper, than after building.

  ANOTHER NOTATION YOU'LL SEE (Chen)
    The older Chen notation draws relationships as DIAMONDS between the boxes, with
    numbers (1, N, M) on the lines, and attributes as ovals. It is common in
    textbooks. You don't need to draw it, only to recognise it: a diamond labelled
    "borrows" between Member and Copy is the same idea as a crow's-foot line.

  TRY IT: on your blank sheet, draw Customer, Order, and Product as three boxes.
    A customer places many orders; each order belongs to one customer. An order
    contains many products, and a product appears on many orders. Draw the lines,
    put the right symbols on the ends, and say all four sentences aloud. You will
    find one line you cannot draw cleanly - hold that feeling; Part 5 is its cure.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  PART 5 - THE PATTERNS BEGINNERS TRIP ON
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Everything so far handles the easy links. A handful of situations need a specific
move each. Learn these five and very little in real design will surprise you.

  5.1  MANY-TO-MANY NEEDS A BRIDGE TABLE  (the big one)
  ----------------------------------------------------
  A student takes many courses; a course holds many students. Try to store this
  with a foreign key and you fail both ways: you cannot list many course-ids in a
  student's single cell, nor many student-ids in a course's. A table cell holds
  one value - so a direct many-to-many is impossible.

  The cure: invent a THIRD table that holds one row per pairing. Call it Enrolment.
  Each row has one student_id and one course_id - two foreign keys - and together
  they identify the row (a composite key, Part 3). Now:
       Student ||--o< Enrolment >o--|| Course
  The impossible M:N has become two easy 1:N links through a table in the middle.
  This helper is called a BRIDGE, JUNCTION, LINK, or ASSOCIATIVE table - same thing.

  THE BONUS: this bridge is the natural home for facts about the PAIRING itself -
  the enrolment date, the final grade, whether it was dropped. Remember from Part
  2.3 that a relationship can have its own attributes; the bridge is WHERE they
  live. When a relationship earns its own facts, it has quietly become an entity.

  >> CONTEMPLATE: your Customer-Order-Product sketch from Part 4. Order-to-Product
     is many-to-many. What is the bridge? (It is the order LINE: one row per "this
     order, this product, this quantity, this price".) Notice the bridge holds
     facts - quantity, price - that belong to neither the order nor the product
     alone. That is the tell that it deserved to exist.

  5.2  ONE-TO-ONE: usually a question, sometimes a choice
  -------------------------------------------------------
  A true 1:1 is uncommon. When you find one, ask first: should these be one
  entity? A Person and their Passport-details might just be more columns on Person.
  But 1:1 splits are made ON PURPOSE when: the extra data is rarely present (keep
  it out of the main table so most rows stay lean), or it is sensitive and kept
  separate for access control, or it is huge (a profile photo) and you don't want
  to drag it along every time you read the main row. So 1:1 = "merge, unless you
  have a reason to split" - and know your reason.

  5.3  SELF-REFERENCING RELATIONSHIPS: trees and hierarchies
  ----------------------------------------------------------
  Sometimes an entity relates to OTHERS OF ITS OWN KIND. An employee has a manager
  who is another employee. A category has a parent category. A comment replies to
  another comment. You model this with a foreign key that points back into the
  SAME table: employee.manager_id points at another employee.id.

  This builds a TREE: a top row whose parent is empty (NULL - the root), rows
  beneath it, rows beneath those, to any depth. It is how you store "folders inside
  folders" shapes with no fixed number of levels. student-panel uses this twice -
  student groups can nest inside groups, and parameters inside parameters - so it
  is worth getting comfortable with now.

  >> CONTEMPLATE: why can't you just make columns parent, grandparent,
     great-grandparent? Because you would have to guess the maximum depth forever,
     and most rows would leave them empty. A single self-pointing parent_id handles
     ANY depth with one column. Recursion beats a fixed guess. (Asking "give me the
     whole branch under this node" then needs a special looping query - a real cost
     of this elegant trick, and a fair thing to weigh.)

  5.4  WEAK / DEPENDENT ENTITIES
  ------------------------------
  Some things cannot exist on their own. An order-line has no meaning without its
  order; a room has no meaning without its building. These are WEAK entities: they
  depend on a "parent" (the strong entity) for their very existence, and often for
  part of their identity too (order-line #3 only makes sense as "line 3 OF order
  #88"). In practice you model a weak entity as its own table with a mandatory
  foreign key to its parent, and usually an ON-DELETE rule that removes the
  children when the parent goes (the lines die with their order). The design
  question a weak entity answers is "can this ever stand alone?" - and the answer
  shapes both its key and its delete behaviour.

  5.5  TERNARY RELATIONSHIPS: prefer an entity in the middle
  ----------------------------------------------------------
  "A supplier supplies a part to a project" links three entities in one fact. You
  CAN draw a three-way relationship, but it is hard to read and harder to add facts
  to. The usual move is the same as for many-to-many: create an entity in the
  middle - a "Supply" row with three foreign keys (supplier, part, project) plus
  its own facts (quantity, price, date). Almost every messy multi-way relationship
  becomes clean once you let the relationship itself become a table.

  KEY IDEA: whenever a link is many-to-many, self-referencing, or carries its own
  facts, the answer is almost always the same shape - a table in the middle whose
  rows are the pairings (or triplings), holding foreign keys and any facts of their
  own. Learn that one move and most "hard" designs become routine.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  PART 6 - NORMALISATION: PUTTING EACH FACT IN ONE PLACE
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Normalisation sounds intimidating and is actually one idea repeated: organise
your tables so that each fact is stored exactly once. Everything below is that
idea, made progressively stricter.

  THE DISEASE: redundancy and its "anomalies"
    Picture one wide table, one row per loan, repeating the member's name, phone,
    and address on every single loan they ever make:

      loan_id | member_name | member_phone | book_title | due_date
      --------+-------------+--------------+------------+---------
      1       | Sara Adib   | 555-1020     | Dune       | ...
      2       | Sara Adib   | 555-1020     | Emma       | ...

    Three illnesses follow, and they are worth naming because you will feel them:
      - UPDATE ANOMALY: Sara changes her phone. You must edit every one of her
        rows; miss one and the database now says two different phones are hers.
      - INSERT ANOMALY: you want to record a new member who hasn't borrowed
        anything yet - but the table is about loans, so there is nowhere to put
        her. Her existence depends on an unrelated fact.
      - DELETE ANOMALY: a member returns their only book and you delete that loan -
        and accidentally erase the only record that the member (and her phone)
        ever existed.
    All three come from ONE mistake: two different KINDS of fact (facts about the
    member, facts about the loan) crammed into one table. The cure is to split
    them so each kind of fact lives in its own table, linked by a key.

  THE CURE, IN STEPS (the "normal forms")
    Each form fixes one kind of muddle. You mostly aim for the third.

    FIRST NORMAL FORM (1NF) - atomic values, no repeating groups.
      Every cell holds ONE value, and no row has "columns" like phone1, phone2,
      phone3. If a thing has many values, it belongs in its own rows/table (the
      multi-valued attribute of Part 2.2, and the bridge of Part 5). Tidy cells.

    SECOND NORMAL FORM (2NF) - no partial dependency on part of a composite key.
      Only bites when your key is composite. In an Enrolment keyed by (student,
      course), the student's NAME depends on the student alone - only part of the
      key - so it does not belong here; it belongs in Student. Every non-key fact
      must depend on the WHOLE key, not half of it.

    THIRD NORMAL FORM (3NF) - no transitive dependency.
      A non-key column must depend on the key, not on ANOTHER non-key column. If a
      Book table stores publisher_id AND publisher_address, the address really
      depends on the publisher, not on the book - so it belongs in a Publisher
      table. Facts about a publisher live with the publisher.

    (There is a stricter cousin, BCNF, and higher forms for rare cases. For almost
    all real work, "clean to 3NF" is the target and the rest is specialist.)

    THE MNEMONIC: every non-key fact must depend on "the key, the whole key, and
    nothing but the key." That single line is 1NF-2NF-3NF in a sentence.

  WHEN TO STOP - and even step back
    Normalisation splits data into more, smaller tables. That is good for
    correctness but means more links to re-join when you ask questions, which can
    be slower. Sometimes, deliberately and knowingly, designers DENORMALISE - keep
    a little redundancy - for speed on read-heavy systems. Do it consciously, never
    by accident, and never before you have a measured reason. Correct first; fast
    later, only if needed.

  >> CONTEMPLATE: go back to the loan table above and split it into clean tables.
     How many do you get, and what is the key and the foreign key of each? (You
     should reach Member, Book, and Loan - the bridge - with Loan carrying member_id
     and book_id. You just normalised a design by yourself.)

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  PART 7 - THE METHOD: A REPEATABLE RECIPE
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Now that you own the pieces, here is the order to use them. Follow it top to
bottom the first few times; later it becomes instinct. Notice it moves from ideas
(steps 1-4) to tables (steps 8-9), exactly the level-1-to-level-2 descent of Part 1.

  STEP 1  STATE THE PURPOSE. Write, in sentences, what the system is for, who uses
          it, and - crucially - the QUESTIONS it must answer and ACTIONS it must
          support (Part 1). This list is your ruler for everything after.

  STEP 2  FIND THE ENTITIES. Underline the NOUNS in your sentences. Group synonyms,
          drop the ones you keep no facts about. What's left are your candidate
          entity types (tables-to-be).

  STEP 3  FIND THE ATTRIBUTES. For each entity, list what you know about it. Mark
          which are composite (split them), which are multi-valued (they will
          become their own table), and which are derived (don't store them).

  STEP 4  FIND THE RELATIONSHIPS. Underline the VERBS joining nouns. For each, ask
          cardinality in BOTH directions and its participation (min/max, Part 2.4).
          Say each relationship aloud as two sentences and check both are true.

  STEP 5  CHOOSE KEYS. Give each entity a primary key (usually a surrogate; keep
          natural identifiers as unique columns). Note where composite keys arise.

  STEP 6  DRAW IT. Put the entities in boxes, draw the relationship lines, mark the
          crow's-foot ends. Now you can SEE the whole model and critique it.

  STEP 7  RESOLVE THE HARD PATTERNS. Turn every many-to-many into a bridge table;
          handle self-references, weak entities, and ternaries (Part 5). Move each
          relationship's own facts onto its bridge.

  STEP 8  NORMALISE. Check each table to 3NF: "the key, the whole key, and nothing
          but the key" (Part 6). Split out any fact that is in the wrong place.

  STEP 9  MAP TO TABLES. Apply the mapping rules below to write the final table
          list: names, columns, types, primary keys, foreign keys, and delete rules.

  STEP 10 REVIEW AGAINST STEP 1. Walk every question and action from your purpose
          list through the design. Can each be answered? Iterate. First drafts are
          never final - good designs are rewritten, not born.

  THE MAPPING RULES (ER model -> tables), collected in one place
    - Each ENTITY becomes a TABLE; each of its atomic attributes becomes a COLUMN.
    - Each entity's PRIMARY KEY becomes the table's primary key.
    - A ONE-TO-MANY relationship becomes a FOREIGN KEY column on the MANY side,
      pointing at the one side's primary key.
    - A ONE-TO-ONE becomes a foreign key on either side (put it on the optional
      side), often with a UNIQUE rule so it stays one-to-one.
    - A MANY-TO-MANY becomes a NEW bridge table whose primary key is the pair of
      foreign keys (plus any facts about the pairing).
    - A MULTI-VALUED attribute becomes its own table, one row per value, linked by
      a foreign key back to the owner.
    - A SELF-REFERENCE becomes a foreign key column pointing back into the same
      table (parent_id), NULL at the root.
    - For each foreign key, decide the ON-DELETE rule: cascade (children die with
      the parent), set-null (link clears, child survives), or restrict (forbid
      deleting while children exist). This encodes what the world allows.

  KEY IDEA: the recipe never changes - purpose, entities, attributes, relationships,
  keys, draw, resolve patterns, normalise, map, review. Memorise the ten steps and
  you can design anything, because you will always know what to do next.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  PART 8 - A COMPLETE WORKED EXAMPLE: A SMALL COMMUNITY LIBRARY
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Let us run the whole recipe once, start to finish, on something small. Read it
with your own sheet and try each step before reading my version.

  STEP 1 - PURPOSE. A small library lends books to members. It must answer:
    "which books does this member have out now?", "is any copy of this title
    available?", "who is overdue?", "how many times has this title been borrowed?"
    Actions: register a member, lend a copy, take a return.

  STEP 2 - ENTITIES (nouns underlined in the purpose): MEMBER, BOOK (the title/work),
    COPY (a physical volume - a title can have several), and - foreseeing a verb
    with its own facts - LOAN. Four entities.

  STEP 3 - ATTRIBUTES.
    MEMBER: id, full name (composite - store given/family), email (unique),
            joined_on. (Age? derived from nothing stored - skip; we don't need it.)
    BOOK:   id, title, author, isbn (natural, unique). (One author, to stay small;
            note the simplification - a real library would make Author an entity.)
    COPY:   id, the book it is a copy of (link), shelf code, acquired_on.
    LOAN:   id, which copy, which member, borrowed_on, due_on, returned_on (NULL
            until returned - its emptiness MEANS "still out").

  STEP 4 - RELATIONSHIPS and their cardinality (say each aloud, both ways):
    BOOK ||--o< COPY      one title has many copies; each copy is of one title.
    MEMBER ||--o< LOAN     a member may have many loans (and zero); each loan, one
                           member.
    COPY ||--o< LOAN       a copy has many loans over time; each loan, one copy.
    Notice LOAN sits at the "many" end of two 1:N links - it is the bridge between
    MEMBER and COPY, and it carries its own facts (the dates). It earned its place.

  STEP 5 - KEYS. Surrogate id on each table (member_id, book_id, copy_id, loan_id).
    Keep email UNIQUE on member and isbn UNIQUE on book as natural identifiers.

  STEP 6 - DRAW IT (in text):
       Member ||---o< Loan >o---|| Copy >o---|| Book
    Read the chain: a member has zero-or-many loans; each loan has exactly one
    copy; a copy has zero-or-many loans and belongs to exactly one book; a book has
    zero-or-many copies. Every sentence is true of a real library - the design holds.

  STEP 7 - HARD PATTERNS. The Member-Copy many-to-many (a member borrows many
    copies over time; a copy is borrowed by many members) is already resolved: LOAN
    is its bridge, and it holds the pairing's own facts (the dates). Nothing else to
    resolve - no self-reference, no ternary here.

  STEP 8 - NORMALISE. Check "the key, the whole key, nothing but the key":
    Loan holds copy_id, member_id, and dates - all facts about THAT loan: good.
    The member's name is NOT on Loan (it depends on the member, not the loan): good.
    The book's title is NOT on Copy (it depends on the book): good. We are at 3NF.

  STEP 9 - THE FINAL TABLES:
    member(member_id PK, given_name, family_name, email UNIQUE, joined_on)
    book(book_id PK, title, author, isbn UNIQUE)
    copy(copy_id PK, book_id FK->book, shelf_code, acquired_on)
    loan(loan_id PK, copy_id FK->copy, member_id FK->member,
         borrowed_on, due_on, returned_on NULL)

  STEP 10 - REVIEW against Step 1's questions:
    "Books this member has out now?" loans where member_id = ? and returned_on is
      NULL, joined to copy and book. Answerable.
    "Is a copy of this title available?" copies of the book with no open loan.
      Answerable. "Who is overdue?" open loans with due_on in the past. Answerable.
    "How many times borrowed?" count loans joined to this book. Answerable. It
    passes - and every table traces back to a real question. That is a finished
    small design, built by the recipe. student-panel is this same method, larger.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  PART 9 - DESIGN JUDGEMENT: THE DECISIONS WITH NO SINGLE RIGHT ANSWER
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The recipe gives you a correct design. Judgement makes it a GOOD one. These are the
recurring trade-offs - the "what could we do differently, and should this exist at
all?" layer. None has one universal answer; each has a question to ask yourself.

  DERIVE vs STORE. If a fact can be computed from others (a total, an age, a count),
    prefer to compute it when asked, so it can never fall out of step. Store it only
    when computing it is genuinely too slow AND you accept the duty to keep it
    fresh. Ask: "can this contradict the facts it is derived from?" If yes, don't
    store it.

  WHAT NULL MEANS. An empty (NULL) value is not zero and not "" - it is "unknown or
    not-applicable." Before you allow a column to be empty, decide what its
    emptiness will MEAN (loan.returned_on being NULL means "still out"). A NULL you
    can't explain in words is a design you haven't finished thinking about.

  SOFT vs HARD DELETE. Deleting a row really removes it (hard). Sometimes you instead
    mark it "deleted_at = now" and hide it (soft delete), so history and references
    survive and mistakes are recoverable. Soft delete keeps the past but complicates
    every query ("...and not deleted"). Ask: "will anyone ever need to know this
    existed?" student-panel soft-deletes students and archives parameters for exactly
    this reason.

  A FLEXIBLE (JSON) COLUMN vs PROPER COLUMNS. Most engines let one column hold a
    free-form bag of data (JSON). It is tempting for "extra, varies-per-row" fields.
    But data in there can't be checked, keyed, or easily searched by the database.
    Rule of thumb: facts you will filter, sort, or enforce deserve real columns;
    only genuinely open-ended extras belong in a flexible column. Reach for it
    rarely and on purpose.

  NAMING. Consistent, boring names are a feature: singular-or-plural table names
    (pick one), id for the primary key, thing_id for a foreign key, lower_snake_case
    throughout. Future-you reads the schema far more often than you write it.

  KEY IDEA: correctness is teachable in ten steps; judgement grows by making these
    calls, stating your reason, and later seeing how it played out. That is exactly
    what our compare-against-the-reference loop is for.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  GLOSSARY - quick reference
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  ENTITY (TYPE)     a kind of thing you keep facts about; becomes a TABLE.
  ENTITY INSTANCE   one specific such thing; becomes a ROW.
  ATTRIBUTE         a fact about an entity; becomes a COLUMN.
    atomic          holds one indivisible value.
    composite       really several values (address -> street, city, postcode).
    multi-valued    has many values at once -> needs its own rows/table.
    derived         computable from other facts -> prefer not to store.
  RELATIONSHIP      an association between entities; the VERB. Degree = how many
                    entity types it joins (unary/binary/ternary).
  CARDINALITY       how many relate to how many: 1:1, 1:N, M:N (the maximum side).
  PARTICIPATION     the minimum side: is zero allowed (optional) or required
                    (mandatory)?
  CANDIDATE KEY     any attribute set that uniquely identifies a row.
  PRIMARY KEY       the one candidate chosen as the official identifier; unique,
                    never empty, ideally never changing.
  NATURAL KEY       a real-world identifier (ISBN, email).
  SURROGATE KEY     a meaningless invented identifier (auto-number, UUID).
  COMPOSITE KEY     a key made of more than one column.
  FOREIGN KEY       a column holding another table's primary-key value; a pointer.
  REFERENTIAL       the database's guarantee that a foreign key points at a real
    INTEGRITY       row (or is empty, if optional).
  BRIDGE / JUNCTION /  a table in the middle that resolves a many-to-many into two
    ASSOCIATIVE table    one-to-manys; holds the pairing's own facts.
  WEAK ENTITY       a thing that cannot exist without its parent (order-line).
  SELF-REFERENCE    a foreign key pointing back into the same table (parent_id);
                    builds trees/hierarchies of any depth.
  NORMALISATION     organising tables so each fact lives in exactly one place.
    1NF             atomic cells, no repeating columns.
    2NF             no non-key fact depends on only PART of a composite key.
    3NF             no non-key fact depends on another non-key fact.
  ANOMALY           an update/insert/delete problem caused by redundancy.
  DENORMALISE       deliberately keep some redundancy for read speed (with care).
  SOFT DELETE       mark a row deleted (deleted_at) instead of removing it.
  NULL              "unknown or not-applicable" - decide what each NULL MEANS.
  CONCEPTUAL /      the three design heights: ideas -> tables -> real storage.
